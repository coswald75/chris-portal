Short Piece · The Cross

The Court of Four Verdicts

Four courts condemned Jesus on Friday. God overturned all four by Sunday.

If you want to understand vindication — what it is, why God loves it so much, why it matters that he promises it to suffering people — you have to start at the one place it looked most impossible. You have to start with a man condemned by everyone at once.

I owe this framing to John Stott, and I’ve found nothing more clarifying. On the cross, Jesus was tried and convicted in four separate courts on the same afternoon.

First, the court of religion. The chief priest asked him directly whether he was the Son of the Most High. He said yes. And the verdict came down: blasphemer. The sentence for blasphemy was death.

Second, the court of the state. Rome was not interested in theology; Rome asked a different question — do you claim to be a king? — and the answer, plainly, was yes. But there is no king but Caesar. Verdict: traitor. The sentence for treason was death.

Third, the court of God. This one was not spoken, it was assumed — by every Jew and every Roman watching. In that world, crucifixion was the single loudest declaration the universe could make that heaven had turned its back on you. To hang on a tree was to be visibly, unmistakably cursed. The verdict, written across the whole event for everyone to read: forsaken by God.

And fourth, the court of nature. Call it the verdict of biology. You need blood. You need oxygen. No one survives crucifixion; it has been tried, exhaustively, and the success rate is zero. Creation itself rendered its judgment: insufficient. This man cannot stay alive.

Four concurrent death sentences — from the Jews, from the Romans, from the Creator, from the creation. There has never in human history been a person who looked more guilty, more foolish, more forsaken than Jesus Christ looked that Friday.

And then God overturned all four by Sunday morning.

Blasphemer? He did not deserve to die for claiming to be God — because he is God. Overturned. Traitor? He did not deserve to die for claiming to be King — because he is the King. Overturned. Forsaken and cursed? He was no sinner being abandoned; he was a Savior bearing our sins, and the verdict reverses into its opposite — he was never more the beloved Son than at that moment. Overturned. And the verdict of nature — that death could hold him — overturned most violently of all, because he is the Lord of life and the grave could not keep its prisoner. In a single weekend the man who looked wrong about everything was exposed, by God himself, as having authority over all of it.

This is why Hebrews can say Jesus endured the cross for the joy set before him (Heb 12:2). We usually fill in that joy with us — and that’s true, he did it to save us. But one of the joys was simpler and more personal than we admit: the joy of being vindicated. He knew Friday’s verdict was a lie. He knew Sunday was coming. Chesterton caught the shape of it — the one perfectly divine thing, the one glimpse of paradise given on earth, is to fight a losing battle and not lose it.

That is the pattern God has woven into reality, and it’s the pattern he promises to repeat in everyone who is in Christ. The verdict the world hands you is not the final verdict. There is a higher court, and it has already shown you how it likes to rule.

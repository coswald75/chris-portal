The Virtues That Boil Off First

The tenderest virtues have the lowest boiling point. Know that before the heat comes.

Here is a piece of self-knowledge worth more than most of what passes for self-knowledge: under pressure, you do not lose your virtues evenly. Some of them are far more volatile than others. Turn up the heat, and certain things in your character boil off almost immediately, while the cheap stuff stubbornly remains.

It’s high-school chemistry, and I confess a lot of us were homeschooled and may have skipped the lab — but you remember the idea of a boiling point. Every substance has a temperature at which it stops being liquid and simply vanishes into the air. The soul is no different. The apostles understood this. They knew that when the heat comes, the question is not will you change but what evaporates first.

And the cruel joke of it is that the worst things in you have the highest boiling point. Reviling, self-pity, sensuality, the convenient lie you tell to justify yourself — those are heat-stable. They hang around. What goes first, what boils off the instant the temperature climbs, is exactly the list Peter names: unity of mind, sympathy, brotherly love, a tender heart, a humble mind (1 Pet 3:8). The tenderest virtues are the most fragile. They are the first to disappear when you are afraid, when you are exhausted, when someone is treating you unjustly. Notice in particular what fear does to humility — it quietly cranks up your certainty about your own perceptions to a level the facts never warranted, so the more frightened you get, the more sure you become that you, and only you, see the situation clearly.

Let me prove how low the boiling point really is, with the cheapest possible heat. Gold-plated marriage counsel: the very first virtue to evaporate under pressure is courtesy. Just ordinary politeness. My wife will laugh reading this, because we moved a refrigerator yesterday — and somewhere under the weight of that thing coming down on me, my courtesy evaporated, and a fair number of other virtues left with it. That was a refrigerator. Not an evil emperor. Not a beating. Not a slander. If a kitchen appliance can boil off my tenderness, I had better know that about myself before any real heat arrives.

Which is the hidden kindness in the whole passage. Peter is warning you in advance, while the room is still cool, exactly what you’ll be tempted toward when it isn’t — disloyalty, unkindness, a self-centeredness for which you’ll find disturbingly persuasive justifications. He’s handing you the map before you’re lost.

And there is precisely one man who walked into the hottest heat that has ever existed and came out with not one virtue evaporated. Father, forgive them, they know not what they do. Today you will be with me in paradise. Every tender thing held. Nothing boiled off. He is the one I’m hiding behind, and the same Spirit who held him steady is offered to you — so that one day, looking back on something that should have undone you, you might say the most wonderful thing a sufferer can say: the virtues didn’t evaporate like they should have — and I don’t think that was me.

Take what’s useful — outline it, preach it, reword it, ignore the rest. No attribution needed.

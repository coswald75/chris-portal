Series Opener · Revelation 1:1

One Way to Open a Series on Revelation

Exegetical spadework and a homiletical engine for launching a series on the Apocalypse.

The governing line

The deepest comfort God gives his people is not an answer to their circumstances but a sight of his delight in his Son. Revelation is the Father’s celebration of the Son, mailed to seven suffering churches as their consolation.

The exegetical spine: Revelation 1:1

Ἀποκάλυψις Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ ἣν ἔδωκεν αὐτῷ ὁ θεός — “The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave to him.”

The genitive question. Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ alone could be subjective (revelation from Jesus) or objective (revelation about Jesus). John settles it in the next clause: ἣν ἔδωκεν αὐτῷ ὁ θεός — God gave it to him. Subjective genitive; Jesus is the receiver and transmitter, not (grammatically) the content. So Beale, Osborne, Mounce, Aune.

The chain of custody. God → Jesus → his angel → John → the servants/churches. Five links, all in verse 1. The angel is not John: ἀποστείλας διὰ τοῦ ἀγγέλου αὐτοῦ τῷ δούλῳ αὐτοῦ Ἰωάννῃ — instrument (διά + gen.) vs. recipient (dative). Confirmed at 22:16 (“I, Jesus, sent my angel to testify to you these things for the churches”) and decisively at 22:8–9, where John falls before the angel and is refused: “I am a fellow servant.”

ἐσήμανεν — “he signified it,” cognate with σημεῖον. The book announces in its first sentence that it speaks in signs. This becomes the standing rebuke to literalist decoding: the book told us it was symbolic before it showed us a single vision.

The convergence. Form and content meet: the revelation comes through Jesus (subjective genitive), but what comes through him is overwhelmingly the Father’s vindication and exaltation of him. This is why some commentators argue a plenary genitive. The syntax says “from”; the book says “about”; both are true and the second is the sermon.

The theological center: Revelation 4–5 re-enacts 1:1

The throne-room drama is the transmission chain of 1:1 staged as liturgy. A scroll in the right hand of the One on the throne; no one worthy; the Lamb takes the scroll from the Father’s hand — and heaven detonates. Three concentric circles of praise: living creatures and elders (5:8–10), myriads of angels (5:11–12), every creature in existence (5:13).

The handing-over of the revelation is not a data transfer. It is an enthronement, and heaven’s response to it is doxology. The Father giving the revelation to the Son and the Father celebrating the Son are the same event.

Johannine backdrop: John 17 (“Father, glorify your Son”), John 3:35 (“the Father loves the Son and has given all things into his hand”). Revelation is that prayer answered in full visionary color.

The homiletical engine: the Transfiguration

Read Matthew 17:1–8 as the introduction to Revelation. The parallels are structural, not decorative — and John was present at both.

Mount Tabor (Matt 17)Patmos (Rev 1)
Face “shone like the sun” (17:2)Face “like the sun shining in full strength” (1:16)
Garments white as lightRobe, golden sash, blazing glory
Disciples fall on their faces (17:6)John falls at his feet as though dead (1:17)
Jesus touches them: “Rise, have no fear” (17:7)Right hand laid on John: “Fear not” (1:17)
“They saw no one but Jesus only” (17:8)The whole book, rightly read

Peter’s error is flattening. Three tents = three comparable figures. Law, Prophets, and Jesus in equivalent shrines — a tidy religious memorial complex. Luke’s mercy note: “not knowing what he said.” Matthew’s detail: Peter was still speaking when the cloud interrupted him. The Father’s answer refuses the arithmetic: not “these are my three servants” but “this is my beloved Son — listen to him.” Then 17:8: Moses and Elijah recede, Jesus only. They are not dishonored; they were always pointing here.

The reader of Revelation fails exactly the way Peter failed. Faced with overwhelming glory, we busy ourselves with the manageable and the fascinating — timelines, charts, locusts-as-attack-helicopters (Hal Lindsey, There’s a New World Coming, on Rev 9), Gog and Magog mapped onto this decade’s headlines. It is tent-building: reverent-looking activity that is actually a way of not looking at the thing itself. Peter’s tents domesticate glory into something he can host and manage; prophecy-chart reading domesticates Revelation into something we can master and predict. Both are ways of standing before the unveiled Son and changing the subject.

The Father’s corrective in both cases is identical, and it is printed as the book’s title: Ἀποκάλυψις Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ. Verse 1 is the voice from the cloud, set in type.

The pastoral logic

The recipients are churches facing Rome, compromise, and blood. What does God send them? Not first a battle plan, not an ethical exhortation, not a decoder ring — a vision of the slain Lamb standing, reigning, opening the seals of history. Their endurance is meant to flow from seeing him enthroned. The comfort offered to the suffering churches is the Father’s celebration of the Son — which is the central comfort of the gospel itself.

2 Peter 1:16–19 gives the template: Peter recalls the Transfiguration as the moment Christ “received honor and glory from God the Father,” and draws the pastoral conclusion — “we have the prophetic word more fully confirmed… a lamp shining in a dark place, until the day dawns and the morning star rises in your hearts.” Who is the morning star? Revelation 22:16: “I, Jesus… am the bright morning star.” The man who babbled on the mountain became the pastor who tells suffering churches to hold the memory of the Father glorifying the Son as their lamp in the dark. That is the job description of the book of Revelation.

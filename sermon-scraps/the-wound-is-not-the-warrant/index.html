Short Piece · Suffering & Care

The Wound Is Not the Warrant

Why excusing the sin of a suffering person is a way of robbing them.

There is a sentence we have all learned to say to people who are hurting, and we say it because it feels like love. We don’t say it out loud in these words, but it’s underneath everything we offer them: you’ve been through enough, so no one expects anything of you now. The wound becomes the warrant. The injury becomes the permission slip — to stop serving, to stop pursuing holiness, to stop doing hard things, to quietly excuse a hundred small unkindnesses because, after all, look what they’ve suffered.

And I want to name why we do it, because the impulse is not stupid. It comes from a real and good place. We have watched people get clobbered by clumsy counsel. We’ve seen the friend who heard about a miscarriage and led with a Bible verse and a fix. We’ve recoiled from that, rightly, and we have overcorrected into a posture where asking anything of a suffering person feels like kicking them. Compassion has quietly redefined itself as the agreement to require nothing.

But watch what the Bible does, because it does not work this way — and not because it’s naive about pain. Peter writes to people who are genuinely, brutally suffering: slandered, reviled, beaten, married to hostile men, living under an emperor who would soon be lighting Christians on fire (1 Pet 1:6; 3:1; 2:18). These are not imagined victims. And Peter never once tells them it isn’t happening, never once tells them it shouldn’t hurt. But he also never hands them the warrant. Instead he calls them up. Unity of mind, sympathy, brotherly love, a tender heart, a humble mind (1 Pet 3:8). Higher virtue, not lower. To real victims.

Why? Because to excuse the sin of a suffering person is to rob them. You think you’re being gentle, and what you’re actually doing is reaching into their future and stealing something. You vacate the joy of overcoming. You vacate the staggering discovery — available only to people who keep walking when they want to quit — of finding inside your own soul a strength for endurance that did not come from your soul. Paul says it plainly: suffering produces perseverance, perseverance produces character, and character produces hope (Rom 5:3–4). Every easy off-ramp you hand a hurting friend in the name of kindness short-circuits that whole engine. You are doing deep damage to their hope, and you are calling it care.

So here is a better posture, and you are allowed to take it. Put your hand on their shoulder and say all of it at once: I’m with you. I’m here. I want to help carry this. And there are about nine ways you could make this worse than it already is — can we talk about those too? Both halves. The presence and the summons. That is not the cold thing. The cold thing is the off-ramp dressed up as mercy.

Take what’s useful — outline it, preach it, reword it, ignore the rest. No attribution needed.

Authority

Submission and Holy Rebellion

Romans 13 · Exodus 6 · Luke 22 · James 4 · The Christian owes real honor to the governing authority and real defiance to the tyranny of sin. Holding both at once is the whole difficulty — and the whole freedom.

Two texts sit in apparent tension at the root of Christian politics. One installs the governing authority as God-ordained and commands honor toward it, even under a Nero. The other — the Exodus — makes resistance to a tyrant into an act of obedience to God. The Christian is asked to hold both, and the holding is not a compromise but a hierarchy: authority is real, and it is penultimate.

Authority is ordained, and it is limited

The apostle grounds the state not in the consent of the governed but in the appointment of God, who installs rulers for the restraint of evil. This is why a believer engages the public order — votes, honors, pays, prays — not from fear and not from partisan allegiance, but from confidence that the outcome is finally God's to give. The gospel, not the ballot, is what shapes both conduct and hope. But the very fact that authority is delegated is what limits it: a power ordained by God cannot demand what God forbids without forfeiting its warrant. Honor the office; refuse its idolatry.

The power to submit

Submission, rightly understood, is not silent resignation. Its model is the King himself in the garden, who submits to the Father through honest struggle rather than passive collapse, and who submits even to unjust human authority by entrusting himself to the One who judges justly. This is the hardest political virtue and the most misused: it is strength, not servility, the deliberate letting-go of our own weapons of control. The believer who learns it can obey a flawed magistrate without fear, because the obedience runs finally to God and not to the man.

Where the true rebellion lies

The Exodus has been the script of every political liberation in the Western memory — Pilgrims, patriots, slaves reading their freedom in Israel's. But its deepest application is not the overthrow of an external Pharaoh. The most pressing tyranny is internal: bondage to sin and to the accuser, from which no revolution can free a people. A nation's outward liberty finally rests on citizens inwardly freed; the rebellion that matters most is the one that renounces the older master. And there is a quieter insurrection the text also names — the presumption that plans as though it were sovereign and judges as though it were God. To live under the kingdom is to lay down that usurped crown too.

Two loyalties, rightly ordered

The Christian is not an anarchist and not a idolater of order. He honors the authority God ordained and defies the tyranny God condemns, and he can tell the two apart because his ultimate allegiance lies elsewhere — with a King already enthroned whose government relativizes every other. That single higher loyalty is what makes both the submission and the rebellion holy.

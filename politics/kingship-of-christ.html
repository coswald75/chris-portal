The Reign

The Kingship of Christ

Psalm 110 · Psalm 24 · Isaiah 9 · Colossians 1 · Every earthly throne is provisional. The New Testament announces a King already enthroned — and once that reign is in view, the politics of every other kingdom is relativized at a stroke.

Isaiah drew the nation with a throne at its center and, for a long stretch of the story, left the seat occupied by men who failed it — by sin, by death, by tyranny. The New Testament announces that the seat is filled at last, and not by another Davidic heir who will disappoint in his turn, but by the eternal Son who took flesh to reign without end. This is the first fact of a Christian politics: not a program, but a King already on the throne.

Enthroned at the right hand

The most-quoted psalm in the New Testament seats its Messiah at God's right hand and puts every enemy under his feet. The reign is not a hope deferred to the end of history; it is a present fact, exercised now from an unshakeable throne. He rules as King with all authority, mediates as Priest after a higher order than Aaron's, and will return as the Warrior who settles every account. A people who know this reign is already underway cannot treat any human government as ultimate; the highest office on earth is a delegated and temporary thing, held under a throne it did not build and cannot topple.

By right of making

The King's claim is total because it rests on creation itself: the earth is his, and everything in it, by right of having made it. There is no neutral ground, no acre outside the deed. But the same psalm that presses the claim also names the terms of approach — clean hands and a pure heart — so that his reign is never bare power. It is holy rule, a kingship that shapes the life of those who own it into worship and mission rather than mere subjection.

Not by coercion but by conversion

Here the kingdom parts ways with every empire Isaiah watched bond corner to corner. Christ advances his rule not by coercion but by conversion — opposing human pride, excluding boasting, exalting the humble, and changing hearts one at a time from the works of the flesh to the fruit of the Spirit. A slave-trader becomes an abolitionist hymn-writer; that is how this throne extends its borders. The kingdom grows the way no earthly power can grow, because its conquest is the remaking of the conquered into willing subjects.

The throne behind the thrones

To confess Christ as King is a political act before it is a private comfort. It says that the decisive government of the world is already settled, that its ruler reigns in perfect justice where every human ruler has failed, and that the grace which secured the throne — bought at the cross, proven at the empty tomb — is the same grace that qualifies rebels to inherit. The citizens of this kingdom live under a reign that no election confers and no coup can end.

Mammon and the Common Life

Luke 12 · Luke 16 · Matthew 6 · Money is the rival god the Bible names most often. How a people holds its wealth — and who it lets die at the gate — is a political and spiritual question at once.

Scripture treats money not as a neutral tool but as a rival lord with a name — Mammon — and a claim on the heart. The kingdom's charter therefore has an economics, and it is as inverted as its honors. How a people holds its wealth, and whom it is willing to let starve at its gate, is a question the gospel refuses to leave to the market.

Covetousness is idolatry

The warning is aimed first at the heart, not the balance sheet: covetousness — the insatiable desire for more — is a subtle idolatry that possesses the rich and poor alike. Our possessions can possess us; what we do with money reveals what we actually worship. This is why the remedy is never mere prudence or budgeting but a change of lord. The gospel frees a person from slavery to greed by unveiling a God of limitless generosity, so that the freed heart stores its treasure where neither moth nor rust nor market can reach it. Generosity is not the tax the kingdom levies; it is the sign that one has been possessed by God instead of by things.

The needy at the gate

The kingdom's economics becomes political the moment another person enters the frame. A rich man feasts while a beggar dies at his door, and the fortunes are eternally reversed — not because wealth is a crime, but because proximity creates responsibility. When the needy are at our gate, indifference is not neutral; it is the very posture that is judged. This is the sober edge of the inverted charter: the least, whom the kingdom blesses, are the ones by whom its citizens are measured.

Rich toward God

Between the idol of accumulation and the sentimentalism that merely pities the poor, the kingdom names a third thing: to be rich toward God through open-handed, self-sacrificial giving to those in need. It is the common life the freed people were bound to at the mountain, worked out in coin. (These themes run on into the work and vocation pages, where the same money is asked a different question.)

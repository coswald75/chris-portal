Leave the Public Square

Thirdwayism

Romans 13 · The third position always whispers the same counsel — leave the public square, file the moral question under 'merely political,' and keep the peace. A reading of thirdwayism, from the Civil War division over slavery to now.

The theology of the two cities is not an abstraction; it is tested in particular nations at particular hours, and the American church has had its hours. One temptation recurs in each of them, and it is worth a name: thirdwayism — the counsel to leave the contested public square, to file the moral question under “merely political,” and to keep the church's peace by keeping its silence. This page reads that temptation at its sharpest — the evangelical division of the Civil War era — not to relitigate a war but to name a posture that outlives it.

A house divided before the war

The great Protestant bodies — Methodist, Baptist, Presbyterian — split over slavery roughly a decade before the nation did. Three positions emerged. Two were open and opposed: the defense of slavery and its abolition. The third presented itself as neutral — the border-state posture that declared slavery a merely political or civil matter, beneath the church's spiritual concern, and so declined to speak. It felt, at the time, like prudence and peace.

The third way, and why it isn't neutral

But a refusal to name a moral question is itself an answer. To call an evil “merely political” and thereby excuse the church from judgment is not to stand above the fray; it is to take a side while claiming not to. This is the sleight of hand at the heart of thirdwayism: it markets retreat as even-handedness. The spiritualized neutrality of the border states was not neutral in its effects, and history has not been kind to it. The lesson is not that the church should baptize a party — the warning against partisan idolatry stands — but that there is a difference between the political questions on which Christians may in good conscience differ and the moral questions on which silence is complicity.

The pattern repeats

The uncomfortable claim is that the same third position is available in every generation, and tempting in each: the move that reclassifies a moral matter as a cultural or political one so that the church may keep its peace and its members. Discernment here is costly and easily counterfeited — zeal masquerades as faithfulness, and cowardice masquerades as prudence. But the history is a warning against the particular counterfeit that dresses retreat in the language of spirituality. The gospel that saves the soul does not excuse the conscience from the neighbor at the gate.

Engaged, and not owned

The way between partisan capture and spiritualized retreat is narrow. It asks Christians to engage the public order without fear and without idolatry — honoring authority, loving the neighbor across every line, and refusing both the party's yoke and the coward's silence — because their true King is not on any ballot and their city is not finally here.

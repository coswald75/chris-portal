The Slander You Never Said Out Loud

Profiles in Contention · No. 1: Simon the Pharisee. An essay series from the Litigating Without a License project.

There's a dinner party in Luke 7 that goes wrong without anyone raising their voice.

A Pharisee named Simon has invited Jesus to eat with him. Somewhere between the reclining and the food, a woman comes in off the street — a woman "of the city," Luke says, which is a polite way of saying everyone at that table knew her reputation. She doesn't say a word. She stands behind Jesus, weeping, and her tears start falling on his feet. Then it gets worse, by the standards of the room: she lets down her hair — a thing respectable women did not do in public — wipes his feet with it, kisses them, and pours perfume over them.

And Simon watches all of this and says something. But not out loud.

"He said to himself, 'If this man were a prophet, he would have known who and what sort of woman this is who is touching him, for she is a sinner'" (Luke 7:39).

One silent sentence. Two verdicts. The woman is condemned — she is a sinner, present tense, case closed. And Jesus is condemned with her — if he were a prophet, he would have known. Simon has quietly ruled against everyone in the room except himself, and dinner continues, and nobody heard a thing.

Here's what I want you to notice first, because everything else depends on it: it's not at all clear that Simon was being malicious.

Read the sentence again. There's no cruelty in it. There's no plot. Simon isn't scheming to destroy the woman or trap Jesus. He is, as far as we can tell, sincerely scandalized. A known sinner is caressing the feet of a visiting rabbi at his table, in his house, in front of his guests. His categories are the respectable categories of his whole religious world. His alarm feels — from the inside — like moral seriousness. If you could have tapped Simon on the shoulder and asked, "Are you slandering two people right now?" he would have been baffled. Slander? I haven't said anything. I'm just seeing what's in front of me.

That's the trap. That's the whole trap.

More than they appear

Because look at what Simon is actually working with. He sees two people: a prostitute and a man claiming to be a prophet. And his data isn't false — her reputation is earned; Jesus really is being talked about as a prophet. Simon's data is something worse than false. It's incomplete, and he has no idea it's incomplete.

She is not just a prostitute. She is a saved sinner — her many sins, Jesus will say before the evening ends, "are forgiven," and the tense of the grammar suggests it had already happened. Those tears aren't a woman trying to purchase something; they're a woman overwhelmed by something already given. And he is not just a man claiming to be a prophet. He is the Savior — the one who did the forgiving. Both of the people Simon is judging are more than they appear to him. And therein lies the basic rub of this whole sin: the person committing slander believes he has all the data, and he doesn't. He is reading a file with the decisive pages missing — and nobody running a silent tribunal ever suspects the file is thin. The confidence and the ignorance arrive together. That's what makes it slander instead of a question.

But go one layer deeper, because Simon's two verdicts are not equally weighted. It looks like his mistake is about the woman. It isn't, fundamentally. His mistake is about Jesus. "She is a sinner" is only the fatal error it is because of who is letting her touch him — because the man at the table has authority to forgive sins and has exercised it. Simon misjudges her past as the whole story precisely because he has misjudged the person who had already rewritten her story. This is why, when Jesus finally turns to him, the question is not "Do you see how harsh you're being?" The question is "Do you see this woman?" — and what that question really means is: Do you see what I have done for this woman? It is a Christological question wearing a pastoral disguise. And it names something true of every slander of a saint, in any century: to pass a verdict on a forgiven person's record is to pass a verdict on what Christ has done with that record. You cannot re-try the woman without re-trying her Savior. Simon thought he was assessing a dinner guest. He was actually ruling on the atonement, early.

Not in a position to see

There's one more layer, and it's the one that should make us most nervous. Simon doesn't just lack data. He lacks the equipment to read the data he has — and he's certain he doesn't.

Watch how Jesus corrects him, because the correction is strange if you're not looking for it. Jesus doesn't open by defending the woman. He opens with an audit of Simon's hospitality: "I entered your house; you gave me no water for my feet... You gave me no kiss... You did not anoint my head with oil" (7:44–46). No water, no kiss, no oil — three ordinary courtesies, all withheld. Why does that belong in a correction about judging?

Because the lovelessness isn't a side issue. It's the optics. Simon assumed he was the one person at that table positioned to see clearly — the sober observer among the compromised. In fact his coldness had already bent his instrument before the woman ever walked in. Affections are part of the seeing apparatus; the man who loves little sees little ("whoever hates his brother... walks in darkness, and does not know where he is going, because the darkness has blinded his eyes," 1 John 2:11 — the log is always in the eye). And the scene proves it in both directions: the woman, loving much, read the room perfectly — she alone knew who was at that table and what he was worth. The man with the theological credentials, loving nothing in the room, misread everyone in it, including himself. Her extravagance was accuracy. His composure was blindness.

Slander without malice

Now jump forward about twenty-five years, to a sentence Paul writes in Romans.

Paul has been preaching a gospel of free grace — justification apart from works, righteousness credited to the ungodly who believe. And a rumor has been traveling ahead of him: "Why not do evil that good may come? — as some people slanderously charge us with saying" (Romans 3:8).

Look at what happened there. Paul's critics heard his actual message — grace, free, for sinners, not earned — and drew what honestly seemed like the obvious inference: this man is teaching that sin doesn't matter. And given their data, the inference was understandable; a gospel of merit-free grace can be misheard that way — so naturally that Paul has to preempt the mishearing again in chapter 6. These critics weren't necessarily lying awake hating Paul. Many were, in their own minds, guarding morality itself. Sincerely scandalized — just like Simon. And just like Simon, their real error was one story up from where they thought it was: they misjudged the message because they had not yet seen what God had done — the same incomplete file, this time with the cross on the missing page.

And Paul calls it slander.

Slander is an event, not a concept

That word choice should stop us, because it breaks the definition most of us carry. We think slander is a category of content — false statements, fabricated facts, malicious lies. But Simon fabricated nothing, and Paul's critics fabricated nothing. The facts, as far as they went, were real.

So here's the better way to hold it: slander is more of an event than a concept. It isn't primarily a kind of sentence; it's a thing that happens — and it happens whenever we approach the data we have with epistemic pride: drawing conclusions while assuming two things that are almost never true — that we have enough data, and that we have the capacity to interpret it properly. Simon had neither. His file was missing its decisive pages (a pardon he hadn't witnessed, a Savior he hadn't recognized), and his interpretive instrument was bent by a coldness he mistook for objectivity. Run data through that machine and slander is what comes out the other side — no malice required. Which means some of the most active slanderers in your church, and in your mirror, are people who would be genuinely wounded to hear the word applied to them, because they never once intended harm. The offense doesn't live in the intention. It lives in the verdict — and in the pride underneath it, the quiet assumption of an understanding that just isn't real.

It starts where nobody can see it

And notice where both of these slanders were fully formed: before they made any sound. Simon's never left his head at all. The verdict against Paul presumably circulated in murmurs before it hardened into a charge. Slander is a finished event in the heart before it is anything in the air — "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks," which means the mouth is downstream. By the time you hear a slander, it's old.

This has two edges, and we need both.

The first: the offense exists even if you never speak it. Jesus responded to Simon's thought. God heard a sentence no human ear ever received, and Luke treats that silent sentence as a real event requiring a real answer. We do not get moral credit for the verdicts we manage not to say. The tribunal in the heart is a genuine courtroom rendering genuine offenses against genuine people — and against the God who has already ruled otherwise on their case ("Who shall bring a charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies").

The second: catching it in the heart is how you prevent the greater damage. A verdict that stays internal wounds two parties — you, and God's assessment of your neighbor. A verdict that goes external wounds without limit; it travels, it recruits, it cannot be recalled. Every whispered reputation-wound you've ever witnessed was once a private thought that nobody caught in time. The heart is not just where slander is born; it's the only place slander is cheap to kill.

What to do with an invisible sin

Here's the hard part: you cannot catch what you cannot see, and the essence of this sin is that it doesn't look like sin from the inside. It looks like discernment. It looks like being a good judge of character. Simon didn't feel malicious; he felt perceptive. So the first move is not resolve — you cannot white-knuckle your way out of verdicts you don't know you're rendering. The first move is a prayer that hands over the search: "Search me, O God, and know my heart; try me and know my thoughts" (Psalm 139:23–24). Only the Holy Spirit can walk into the sealed courtroom and turn the lights on. That is, not incidentally, part of his job description — the Advocate convicts precisely so that cases get closed rather than prosecuted.

And then watch how Jesus handles Simon, because it's the model for how he handles us. He doesn't blast him. He tells him a story — two debtors, one forgiven little, one forgiven much — and asks a question Simon can't get wrong: "Which of them will love him more?" Then the audit of the missing courtesies, gentle and devastating. And then the question the whole evening was built to ask — Do you see this woman? — which is to say: Do you see what I have done for this woman? He offers Simon the missing pages of the file. He re-reads the entire evening's data in front of everyone — the tears, the hair, the perfume — as evidence not of shamelessness but of a forgiveness already received. Same facts. True verdict. The difference between Simon and Jesus at that table was never information about the woman's past. It was that one of them knew what grace had done, and the other assumed nothing had happened.

Which is the cure, in the end. The person most dangerous with a verdict is the person who has forgotten being acquitted. "He who is forgiven little, loves little" — and Simon's deepest data problem wasn't the woman's file, or even Jesus' file. It was his own. He'd never sat where she sat, so he read her tears from the gallery instead of from the defendant's chair.

You will hold a silent tribunal this week. Probably today. The gavel will feel like discernment, the verdict will feel obvious, and no one will hear a thing. So pray the dangerous prayer before you need it: Search me. Show me the sentence I'm about to say to myself. Because the event is real even when it's silent — and it rarely stays silent.

And when the Spirit shows you the file with the missing pages, remember what the question at Simon's table was actually asking, and ask it about whoever is standing in your courtroom:

Do you see what I have done for this one?

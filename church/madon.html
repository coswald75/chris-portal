Madon (מָדוֹן) — Strife, Contention

Part of Litigating Without a License.

Definition: Strife, contention, quarreling — specifically, dispute with a legal flavor. From the root דין (din): to judge, to plead a case, to litigate. A madon is not a mood; it is a case being pressed. The contentious person is running perpetual litigation.

Word family

Form Hebrew Notes
madon מָדוֹן The noun, singular — "strife"
midyanim / medanim מִדְיָנִים / מְדָנִים Plural forms — "contentions" (intensive: contentions upon contentions). Several occurrences carry a ketiv/qere where the written form resembles "Midianites" (מדינים)
din דִּין The root noun/verb — judgment, legal cause, lawsuit. See Din
riv רִיב The nearest synonym — lawsuit, legal dispute; paired with madon twice. See Riv

Every occurrence

Concentrated overwhelmingly in Proverbs (the singular madon ~13×; plural forms add another ~10):

What produces it: - Prov 10:12 — hatred stirs up contentions (medanim); love covers all offenses - Prov 15:18 — a man of wrath stirs up madon; the slow to anger quiets a riv - Prov 16:28 — a dishonest man spreads madon; a whisperer separates close friends - Prov 28:25 — a greedy man (lit. "wide of appetite") stirs up madon - Prov 29:22 — a man of anger stirs up madon; a man of wrath abounds in transgression - Prov 6:14, 19 — the worthless man devises evil, "continually sowing contentions"; the LORD hates "one who sows contentions among brothers" - Prov 23:29 — who has woe, sorrow, midyanim, complaining? Those who tarry long over wine - Prov 26:20 — where there is no whisperer, madon ceases — gossip is the fuel (see Slander, Gossip, and the Busybody)

Who embodies it: - Prov 21:9; 25:24 — the eshet midyanim, "woman of contentions"; better the corner of the roof. See Eshet Midyanim - Prov 21:19 — better a desert land than an eshet midyanim who is also fretful - Prov 27:15 — her quarreling is a continual dripping on a rainy day - Prov 19:13 — the same word used of the dripping itself - Prov 26:21 — the male counterpart: the ish midyanim ("quarrelsome man") is charcoal to embers and wood to fire

How it behaves: - Prov 17:14 — "the beginning of madon is like letting out water; so quit before the riv breaks out" — a breached dam: stoppable at the seep, not at the flood - Prov 18:19 — a brother offended is harder to win than a strong city; midyanim are like the bars of a castle — contention locks - Prov 22:10 — "drive out a scoffer, and madon goes out, and din and abuse cease" ⚖️ - Prov 18:18 — "the lot puts an end to midyanim and decides between powerful contenders" ⚖️

Outside Proverbs (all three carry the courtroom flavor): - Jer 15:10 ⚖️ — Jeremiah: "woe is me, my mother, that you bore me, a man of riv and a man of madon to the whole land! I have not lent, nor have I borrowed, yet all of them curse me" - Hab 1:3 ⚖️ — "riv and madon arise" — followed immediately by 1:4: "the law is paralyzed, and justice never goes forth" - Ps 80:6 — "You make us an object of madon to our neighbors" — God imposes it as judgment

(Textual curiosity: 2 Sam 21:20's "man of great stature" is written איש מדון — but the parallel in 1 Chr 20:6 shows it's a homograph of מִדָּה, "measure," not our word.)

Are there positive uses?

Of the noun madon itself — no. Every occurrence treats it as a fire, a flood, a dripping, a prison bar, or a curse. Proverbs never says "blessed is the man of madon." The closest to neutral or legitimate:

  1. Prov 18:18 — the lot "puts an end to midyanim between powerful contenders." Here the contention is a real dispute needing adjudication — two strong parties with claims — and the proverb's interest is in a God-referencing mechanism (the lot, cf. 16:33) that closes the case. This is madon inside an actual legal process, and the wisdom is in ending it.
  2. Jer 15:10 — the one arguably righteous bearer of the title. Jeremiah is an ish madon "to the whole land" not because he is quarrelsome but because God's word makes him contend with everyone. His protest — "I have not lent, nor have I borrowed" — is creditor-debtor litigation language: he's treated like the town's most litigious man though he has no suits to file. The prophet as necessary contender. (Preaching caution: even here madon is Jeremiah's affliction, not his virtue — the verse is a lament.)
  3. Ps 80:6 — God is the acting subject: He makes Israel a madon to her neighbors. Not positive, but sovereignly wielded.

The clear courtroom uses

The litigation flavor of the din root surfaces explicitly in four places (marked ⚖️ above):

  • Prov 22:10 pairs madon directly with דִּין (din) used in the sense of lawsuit: expel the scoffer and "strife goes out, and litigation and abuse cease." The verse itself treats madon and lawsuits as one package.
  • Prov 18:18 sets midyanim in a formal dispute-resolution scene: contenders, claims, and a verdict by lot.
  • Jer 15:10 pairs ish riv ("man of lawsuit") with ish madon, then reaches for lending/borrowing — the classic occasion of ancient litigation (cf. Exod 22:25ff; Neh 5).
  • Hab 1:3–4 places riv and madon inside a portrait of a failed justice system: paralyzed torah, justice (mishpat) that "never goes forth," the wicked surrounding the righteous "so justice goes forth perverted." Madon is what fills the streets when the true court fails.

Where the root turns positive

Madon is the root gone sour; דין (din) itself is redeemed whenever the right judge holds the court:

  • Prov 31:8–9 — "open your mouth for the mute... judge righteously (דִּין), defend the rights (דִּין) of the poor and needy." Same root, same chapter that ends with the excellent wife — Lemuel's mother teaches the redeemed use of the contentious faculty: open your mouth for the voiceless, not against your neighbor.
  • Jer 22:16 — "he judged (dan) the cause (din) of the poor and needy... Is not this to know me? declares the LORD"
  • Gen 30:6 — "God has judged me (dananni)... and given me a son" — Dan named for vindication
  • Ps 54:1 — "vindicate me (tedineni) by your might"
  • And the synonym riv, when God takes it up: "the LORD will plead their cause (yariv rivam)" — Prov 22:23; 23:11 ("their Redeemer is strong"); Jer 50:34; Mic 7:9; 1 Sam 24:15

The wiki's thesis in one line: the din faculty — the capacity to weigh, charge, and plead — is good when God, the king, or the advocate of the poor wields it, and becomes madon the moment it is self-appointed and self-serving. The critical spirit is a real courtroom gift practicing without a license — rehearsed first in the silent tribunal, and patterned on the Accuser himself.

Imagery cluster

Proverbs never argues about madon; it pictures it: water through a breach (17:14), fire and fuel (26:20–21), the dripping roof (19:13; 27:15), prison bars (18:19), the shared house made unlivable (21:9; 25:24). Every image is of something small that compounds — a seep, a spark, a drip — which is precisely the internal-verdict point: the case is winnable only before it's audible.

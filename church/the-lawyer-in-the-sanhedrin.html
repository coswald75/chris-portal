The Lawyer in the Sanhedrin

Profiles in Contention · No. 3 — Section I: The Man Against the Mob. An essay series from the Litigating Without a License project.

The most important piece of furniture in American literature might be a chair.

It's the night before Tom Robinson's trial in To Kill a Mockingbird, and Atticus Finch has quietly taken a kitchen chair and a lamp down to the Maycomb jailhouse and set them up in front of the door. He's reading a newspaper. He knows what's coming, and it comes: cars in the dark, and men getting out of them — ordinary men, farmers, neighbors — arranged now into that thing which is more and less than the sum of its members. A mob has arrived to take the prisoner, and between the mob and the cell sits one lawyer in a chair, with a lamp, holding no weapon but his refusal to move.

What breaks the standoff isn't Atticus. It's his eight-year-old daughter, who slips through the crowd before anyone can stop her and starts talking to one of the men — by name. "Hey, Mr. Cunningham." She asks about his entailment, his son Walter, school. And under the weight of being personally, individually known, Walter Cunningham stops being a mob and becomes a man — embarrassed, human, decent — and tells everyone to go home. It is the same medicine Moses administered at the gate of the calf-camp in our last essay: a mob dissolves the moment someone refuses to address it as a mob. Scout de-mobbed a lynch party by turning it back into neighbors, one name at a time.

But the chair is the image to keep. Because the next day Atticus does the same thing in a courtroom that he did at the jailhouse door: he places himself, calmly and without illusions, between a pre-rendered verdict and an innocent man.

A case lost before it started

Understand what Atticus is actually up against, because it is not a shortage of evidence. The evidence is fine — for the defense. He demonstrates to the jury that the crime, as described, was almost certainly committed by a left-handed man, and that Tom Robinson's left arm is useless. The town's verdict does not rest on facts. It rests on a frame that decides in advance what the facts are allowed to mean: a white woman has testified, and the accused is a Black man, and in Maycomb in the 1930s that arithmetic completes itself. It is — to use this project's language — a verdict defensible with the data and false: real testimony, really given, meaning something other than the truth. Atticus tells his brother early on that he's "licked" before he starts. He defends Tom anyway, on the grounds that being beaten a hundred years before you begin is no excuse for not trying to win.

Now — the question this series keeps asking. Where is this man in the Bible?

"Does our law judge a man without a hearing?"

He's in John chapter 7, and he has a name: Nicodemus.

You remember him as the night visitor of John 3 — the Pharisee, the "teacher of Israel," the member of the Sanhedrin who came to Jesus in the dark with his questions. Cautious, then. But four chapters later the council is in full cry. The officers have come back empty-handed from arresting Jesus, the chief priests are furious, and the room has reached its ruling without a trial: "Has any of the authorities believed in him? But this crowd that does not know the law — they are accursed." The verdict is in. The frame has decided what the facts mean. And one member of the court stands up with a chair-at-the-jailhouse question:

"Does our law judge a man without first giving him a hearing and learning what he does?" (John 7:51)

That is Atticus Finch's entire jurisprudence in nineteen words. Nicodemus does not say "Jesus is the Messiah" — he isn't there yet. He says something both smaller and mightier: the law requires process. Hear him first. Learn the facts first. He is invoking the oldest safeguards in his people's code — "hear the cases between your brothers, and judge righteously" (Deuteronomy 1:16); "inquire diligently" (Deuteronomy 17:4); and behind them the commandment we met last essay, the one written against exactly this room: you shall not fall in with the many... siding with the many, so as to pervert justice (Exodus 23:2). One lawyer, in a Sanhedrin that has already voted in its heart, asking the court to be a court.

And listen to the answer he gets: "Are you from Galilee too?"

They don't rebut him. They reassign him. In a prejudice-driven prosecution, the advocate is always relocated into the defendant's category — that is how the mob punishes defection. Maycomb had an ugly word for Atticus, and they made sure his children heard it; the Sanhedrin's version was Galilean — hick, sympathizer, one of them. The moment you defend the accused, you join the accused. Every advocate in history has paid this toll, and it is worth saying plainly: the slur aimed at the defender is involuntary testimony that the court has stopped judging cases and started judging categories.

Losing

Here is what keeps this from being a feel-good story, and what makes the parallel hold all the way down: both lawyers lose.

Atticus loses. The jury convicts Tom against the evidence, and Tom — despairing of a system that was never going to acquit him — dies. And Nicodemus loses. The hearing he demanded never happens. When the court finally gets its defendant, it convicts him at night, on suborned testimony, with the sentence decided before the gavel — a proceeding that violated every safeguard Nicodemus had invoked, the mob's masterpiece dressed in judicial robes. His colleague Joseph of Arimathea — "a good and righteous man... who had not consented to their decision and action" (Luke 23:50–51) — could do no more, in the end, than have his dissent noted for the record.

But watch where the two lawyers go after their verdicts, because this is the deepest beat in both stories. Atticus, the morning after the trial, is at the Robinson home, standing with the family — the advocate who stays when the case is lost. And Nicodemus? The night visitor, the cautious proceduralist, chooses the single most dangerous week in Jerusalem's history to go public: "Nicodemus also, who earlier had come to Jesus by night, came bringing a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about seventy-five pounds" (John 19:39). Seventy-five pounds — a royal burial's worth — hauled openly to the corpse of a man his own court had just executed, in company with Joseph, who walked into Pilate's palace and asked for the body by name. The timid man of chapter 3 vanished somewhere between the verdict and the tomb. Advocacy that depends on winning is only strategy. Advocacy after the verdict is love.

There is a smaller, stranger figure worth setting beside them both. When the officials' mob sank the prophet Jeremiah into a muddy cistern to starve, one man went to the king for him — Ebed-melech, a Cushite, a foreigner in the court, as out-of-category as an advocate can be. He got his warrant, gathered thirty men, and hauled the prophet up with ropes — and first, Scripture pauses to tell us, he threw down "old rags and worn-out clothes" for Jeremiah to put under his armpits, so the ropes wouldn't cut (Jeremiah 38:11–12). Rags under the ropes. That detail is the whole anatomy of the advocate — rescue with the tenderness engineered in — and it is a chair and a lamp at a jailhouse door, twenty-five centuries early. God, for the record, did not forget him (Jeremiah 39:15–18).

The jury was full of churchgoers

Now the turn, because this essay is not allowed to be flattering.

Every reader of To Kill a Mockingbird finishes the book certain of one thing: I would have been Atticus. Nobody reads it and identifies with the jury. But the jury is who most of us would have been — twelve ordinary men, family men, hymn-singing men, not a monster among them, every one persuaded that he was seeing clearly and voting sensibly. The mob at the jailhouse went home ashamed; the jury did the same work the next day in clean shirts and felt fine. And Jesus — who watched his own court assemble — left a warning aimed precisely at our certainty that we'd have dissented: "If we had lived in the days of our fathers, we would not have taken part with them in shedding the blood of the prophets" (Matthew 23:30). He calls that sentence the tell. The confidence that you would have been the lone dissenter is the surest sign of the juror. The only reliable evidence either way is how you vote in the courtroom you're actually seated in — the quiet one, the daily one, the one where a verdict about your spouse or your pastor or that family in the church is forming right now with no Nicodemus in the room, unless it's you.

Stand up

One scene remains, and it's the one the essay has been saving.

The courtroom empties after the verdict. Atticus packs his briefcase alone and walks up the center aisle, beaten. And in the balcony — the segregated gallery where Tom's people watched the whole doomed trial — every person rises to their feet in silence. Scout, sitting among them, doesn't understand until Reverend Sykes touches her shoulder: "Miss Jean Louise, stand up. Your father's passin'."

They stood for the advocate who lost. They knew the verdict was wrong, and they knew what it had cost him to fight it, and honor was the only sentence left in their jurisdiction — so they rose.

There was another Advocate who lost. His procedural objections went unraised because his one procedural friend was outvoted; his trial was held at night; his jury was unanimous; his sentence was executed by sundown. He lost his case — on purpose, the record later showed, as the case: the innocent convicted so that the guilty could be acquitted, the Advocate absorbing the verdict his clients deserved. And on the third day the court of heaven reversed the ruling and then did something better than reverse it — seated him permanently for the defense: "if anyone sins, we have an Advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous" (1 John 2:1). He is, at this hour, mid-argument on behalf of everyone who has ever sat in Tom Robinson's chair before the law of God — with this difference, which is the gospel: we were guilty as charged, and he pleads his own blood, and he has never lost since.

One day the gallery of everyone he ever defended rises — every Tom, every Jeremiah in the mud, every defendant the mobs of history got to first. Not in mourning, the way Maycomb's balcony stood. In welcome.

Stand up. Your Advocate is passing.

Ish Midyanim — The Contentious Man: Accuser or Advocate

Part of Litigating Without a License. The facing page to Eshet Midyanim. Proverbs 26:21: "As charcoal to hot embers and wood to fire, so is a quarrelsome man (ish midyanim) for kindling strife" — filed one verse after the whisperer (26:20), because the fuel and the fire-starter work the same job site. Scripture assigns each sex its native dialect of Madon: hers clusters around speech-and-circuit, his around anger and rivalry — but it is one language.

The texts

  • Prov 26:21 — the ish midyanim: charcoal to embers, wood to fire — he doesn't create grievances; he ignites whatever is lying around
  • Prov 15:18 — "a man of wrath (ish chemah) stirs up strife (madon), but he who is slow to anger quiets contention (riv)"
  • Prov 29:22 — "a man of anger (ish af) stirs up strife, and a man of wrath abounds in transgression" — the contention is never his only file
  • Prov 22:24–25 — quarantine order: "make no friendship with a man given to anger... lest you learn his ways and entangle yourself in a snare" — contentiousness is contagious; the charcoal wants company
  • Prov 28:25 — "a greedy man (lit. wide of appetite) stirs up strife" — appetite is a litigator
  • Prov 22:10 — "drive out a scoffer, and strife (madon) goes out, and lawsuits (din) and abuse cease" — sometimes the fire code is expulsion
  • Prov 6:12–19 — the ish belial, the worthless man: crooked speech, winking eyes, signaling feet, "with perverted heart devises evil, continually sowing contentions (midyanim)" — climaxing in the seventh thing the LORD hates: "one who sows discord among brothers" (see The Internal Courtroom — the heart is the factory)
  • Jer 15:10 — the one righteous bearer: Jeremiah, ish riv and ish madon to the whole land — contentious by calling, not by temperament (see Madon)

The engine: threatened glory

The eshet midyanim's engine is characteristically fear — fear manages what it cannot entrust (Eshet Midyanim; Accusing God). The ish midyanim runs on the twin fuel: pride and envy — threatened glory prosecuting its rivals.

  • Prov 13:10 — "By insolence (zadon) comes nothing but strife" — pride is not one cause among several; Proverbs says nothing but
  • Prov 20:3 — "It is an honor for a man to keep aloof from strife, but every fool will be quarreling" — the proverb is aimed at honor-culture: the man who thinks his honor requires the fight is told that honor is in the aloofness
  • James 4:1–2 — "What causes quarrels (machai)?... your passions... you desire and do not have, so you murder. You covet and cannot obtain, so you fight"
  • 1 Sam 18:6–9 — the case study ignition: the women's song is accurate data ("Saul has struck down his thousands" — he had), but Saul renders the rival-verdict: "what more can he have but the kingdom? And Saul eyed David from that day on." Envy is a quarrel with God's allocation of glory — the masculine twin of the fearful woman's quarrel with God's provision. Both are suits against providence, executed against a nearby human
  • Matt 27:18 — how far the engine drives: "he knew that it was out of envy that they had delivered him up." The crucifixion's proximate human motive is this page's sin
  • 1 John 3:12 — Cain murdered "because his own deeds were evil and his brother's righteous" — the first murder is an adverse verdict resented
  • 1 Tim 6:4–5 — Paul's complete anatomy of the contentious man, in causal order: "puffed up with conceit... an unhealthy craving for controversy and quarrels about words (logomachiai), which produce envy, dissension, slander (blasphēmiai), evil suspicions (hyponoiai ponērai), and constant friction." Pride → word-wars → envy → slander → evil suspicions — the internal courtroom appears in a Pauline vice list as the terminal product of male contentiousness. Note that doctrinal contention is the specifically respectable male on-ramp (cf. the Vogan confession on The Internal Courtroom: "I discovered the doctrines of grace and was marked by anything but grace")

The gallery: men accusing

  • Cain (Gen 4) — the original. An adverse verdict received ("for Cain and his offering he had no regard"), the internal court convenes ("Cain was very angry, and his face fell"), and God cross-examines the tribunal before it acts: "Why are you angry?... sin is crouching at the door." The first murder in history is an executed verdict from an unlicensed court
  • Korah (Num 16:3) — the data-defensible charge in ecclesial form: "all the congregation are holy" — which Exod 19:6 says — "why then do you exalt yourselves above the assembly of the LORD?" True premise, false verdict, stolen jurisdiction; the ground's opening is God's ruling on the unlicensed prosecution of his servants
  • Saul (1 Sam 18–22) — the paranoid tribunal at full term: envy (18:9) plus fear (18:12, "Saul was afraid of David because the LORD was with him") maturing into a court where self-pity is the prosecutor: "all of you have conspired against me... no one is sorry for me" (22:8) — and then the Nob massacre on a single informant's tip. ("He's the king of Israel... and he's the one that's jealous of a shepherd boy turned general... You don't even get home from war before Saul's heart begins to turn." — Ricky Alcantar, Jealous?, 2025-11-23)
  • Doeg (1 Sam 21–22; Psalm 52) — the male slanderer with his own psalm. Mark this: Doeg's report was factually true — Ahimelech did give David bread and the sword. Data accurate, framing lethal, context withheld — definition-slander with a body count of eighty-five priests. David's verdict on him: "Your tongue plots destruction, like a sharpened razor, you worker of deceit... you love evil more than good" (Ps 52:2–3)
  • Shimei (2 Sam 16:5–13) — the curbside prosecutor: "Get out, you man of blood!" — data-defensible (Saul's house did fall as David rose), frame false (David had spared Saul twice and executed Saul's killers). David's response is the model for receiving accusation: riv-transfer — "Leave him alone... It may be that the LORD will look on the wrong done to me"
  • Sanballat and Tobiah (Neh 6:5–9) — slander at the institutional scale, with the epistemology showing: "It is reported among the nations... that you and the Jews intend to rebel" — gossip citing gossip as its source. Nehemiah's two-sentence protocol: deny precisely ("no such things as you say have been done, for you are inventing them out of your own minds") and pray immediately ("But now, O God, strengthen my hands"). Refute; don't descend; re-file at the bench
  • The elder brother (Luke 15:25–32) — the respectable accuser: prosecutes his brother with invented data ("devoured your property with prostitutes" — a datum he could not possess; the far country kept no receipts for him) and indicts the father in the same breath ("you never gave me a young goat") — accuser of brother and of the God-figure in one speech, delivered standing outside the feast. The parable ends with the advocate-father coming out to entreat the accuser — and Jesus leaves him at the door, mid-decision
  • Diotrephes (3 John 9–10) — proof that the "feminine" sin wears a collar: "likes to put himself first... talking wicked nonsense against us (phlyarōn — the same word as the "gossips" of 1 Tim 5:13, in the masculine, in church office) ... and puts them out of the church." The gossip circuit run from the moderator's chair, with excommunication as its gavel
  • Daniel's accusers (Dan 6:4–5) — jurisdiction manufactured to order: "We shall not find any ground for complaint against this Daniel unless we find it in connection with the law of his God" — when the record is clean, the unlicensed court legislates a new crime. The lions apply the measure-principle

The gallery: men advocating

  • Jonathan (1 Sam 19:4–5; 20) — the narrative flagship, Abigail's mirror. He "spoke well of David to Saul his father" — advocacy delivered to the accusing power, at cost, against his own dynastic interest (the man he defends will take his throne, and he knows it, 23:17). His brief is a model: character testimony ("he has not sinned against you"), evidence review ("he took his life in his hand and struck down the Philistine"), and a warning aimed at the accuser's soul ("Why then will you sin against innocent blood?") — like Abigail, he advocates for the king against the king. And he absorbs the spear meant for the defendant (20:33)
  • Paul for Onesimus (Philemon) — the epistolary flagship: a whole New Testament book that is a single advocacy brief. "I appeal (parakalō) to you for my child Onesimus" (v. 10 — the paraclete verb); "receive him as you would receive me" (v. 17 — imputed standing); "if he has wronged you at all, charge that to my account... I will repay" (vv. 18–19 — imputed debt, in Paul's own handwriting). A human advocate practicing the exact courtroom mechanics of the gospel on behalf of a runaway slave with a genuinely bad file (see The Advocate (Paraklētos))
  • Moses (Num 12:13) — accused by his own siblings, he responds by interceding for the accuser: "O God, please heal her — please." The advocate's reflex under fire; cf. "stood in the breach" (Ps 106:23)
  • Joseph (Gen 50:19–21) — the jurisdiction question answered rightly, by the one man with a perfect case: "Am I in the place of God?" He holds the evidence, the power, and the grievance — and declines the seat, re-reading the data under God's verdict: "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good"
  • Boaz (Ruth 2–4) — advocacy in both registers: Barnabas-style affirmation over Ruth ("The LORD repay you for what you have done... a full reward be given you," 2:11–12 — observed grace, called out) and then the licensed court itself — the gate scene of Ruth 4, where the go'el executes redemption through exact due process, witnesses and all. Redemption is courtroom work done right
  • Nicodemus and Joseph of Arimathea — the dissenting councilmen: "Does our law judge a man without first giving him a hearing?" (John 7:50–51); "he had not consented to their decision and action" (Luke 23:50–51). When the licensed court goes rogue, advocacy looks like procedural objection and recorded dissent — and eventually like carrying the body (John 19:38–42)
  • Nehemiah, David, Job, Stephen, Barnabas, Gamaliel — the bench of the already-cited: deny-and-pray (Neh 6), spare-and-transfer (1 Sam 24; 2 Sam 16), pray-for-the-prosecutors (Job 42:8–10; Acts 7:60), advocate-against-defensible-suspicion (Acts 9:27), and procedural restraint (Acts 5:34–39)

Office and household: the male qualifications

The mirror of 1 Tim 3:11's mē diabolous (see Eshet Midyanim): the men's office list targets the male dialect —

  • 1 Tim 3:3 — the overseer: "not violent but gentle, not quarrelsome (amachos)" — literally not a fighter. The women's list bars the accuser's mouth; the men's bars the accuser's fists and temper. Same disqualifier, native dialect
  • Titus 1:7 — "not arrogant or quick-tempered" — the engine itself (pride, anger) named in the qualification
  • 2 Tim 2:24–26 — the working job description: "the Lord's servant must not be quarrelsome (amachon) but kind to everyone... correcting his opponents with gentleness" — and note where the passage lands: "that they may come to their senses and escape from the snare of the devil." The gentle corrector is doing rescue work against the diabolos; the quarrelsome one is doing the devil's work in the Lord's name
  • 1 Tim 2:8 — the gendered corrective in worship: men are to pray "lifting holy hands without anger or quarreling" — Paul's assumption about what men bring into the prayer meeting is exactly this page

The page in one line

The ish midyanim is threatened glory prosecuting its rivals — charcoal looking for embers, pride looking for a logomachia, envy looking for a Nob; the advocate-man is Jonathan pleading his replacement's innocence to the king's face, and Paul signing his own name under another man's debt.

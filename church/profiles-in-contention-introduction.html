Profiles in Contention — An Introduction to the Series

Essay series from the Litigating Without a License project.

In the fall of 1954, a young senator from Massachusetts was flat on his back in a New York hospital, recovering from spinal surgery that nearly killed him. The back had been ruined, in part, eleven years earlier in the Solomon Islands, on a night when a Japanese destroyer came out of the dark at forty knots and cut his boat — PT-109 — in half. John F. Kennedy spent the hours after the collision in the water hauling a badly burned crewman toward an island, the man's life-jacket strap clenched in his teeth. He came home a decorated hero with a body that never stopped charging him interest for it.

And in that hospital bed, unable to walk, he wrote a book about a different kind of nerve. Profiles in Courage told the stories of eight United States senators who, at the decisive hour, defied their party, their constituents, and their own careers to do what they believed was right. It won the Pulitzer Prize, and its most famous chapter is worth pausing on, because it happens — of all places — in a courtroom. In 1868, Senator Edmund Ross of Kansas walked into the impeachment trial of President Andrew Johnson holding the deciding vote. The mob, the newspapers, and his entire party demanded conviction. Ross looked down, as he later put it, "into my open grave" — and voted to acquit, because the case hadn't been proven. It ended his career. Kennedy profiled him as the bravest of them all: one man refusing to join a unanimous accusation.

Hold that image. We'll need it for the whole series.

Now, a point of personal business. My last name is Oswald. Kennedy men and Oswald men have — how to put this delicately — a strained history, and it is not the Kennedys who owe the apology. So consider this series a small act of literary homage across the enmity of our surnames: an Oswald, for once, aiming at what a Kennedy built and hoping to honor it. It seems the least my name can do.

Because I want to borrow his format for the opposite subject.

Kennedy profiled the rare men who kept their heads when the crowd demanded otherwise. But the very rarity of his subject tells you something about the rest of us. For every Edmund Ross there were fifty-three senators voting with the mob, a gallery howling for a verdict, and a nation of editorial pages happily supplying one. Courage got a book because courage is scarce. What's common — common as breathing — is the thing courage resists: the rush to a verdict. The whispered case. The certainty that we've seen enough to rule. Scripture has an old word for it: madon — contention, but contention of a particular flavor, contention with a courtroom in it. The quarrel that is really a prosecution. The Bible's blunt claim, which this series will keep testing against its own characters, is that every one of us runs a quiet courtroom of the heart — hearing cases against our spouses, our leaders, our rivals, and ultimately against God — and that in the courtroom of everyone you know, only two speaking roles remain: the accuser, and the advocate.

So: Profiles in Contention. A series of essays, each built around one biblical figure caught in the act — of prosecuting, or of defending. This is Scripture's own teaching method, incidentally. The Bible does not hand us a lecture on envy; it hands us Saul with a spear. It does not define demagoguery; it stands Absalom at the gate, kissing the litigants. Paul says plainly that these stories "happened to them as an example, but they were written down for our instruction" (1 Corinthians 10:11). And the Bible even wrote the original profile series — Hebrews 11 is Profiles in Faith, a roll call of the commended. This series walks the same portrait gallery and studies the other wall: the accusers who divide us, and beside each of them, wherever Scripture supplies one, the advocate who shows the better way. Michal at the window, and Abigail on the road. Saul with the spear, and Jonathan pleading for his rival. Korah with his true premise and false conclusion. Naomi calling herself empty with her treasure standing beside her. The elder brother outside the music. And the profiles will not be sneering ones — that's a promise and a discipline — because the unnerving discovery in nearly every case is that the accuser wasn't twirling a mustache. He was sincere. She was scandalized. They thought they were the ones seeing clearly. Which is precisely what makes their company so uncomfortable: we keep finding our own reflection in the portrait.

The first profile is already hung. It's a dinner party in Capernaum, a weeping woman, and a host who never said a word out loud: The Slander You Never Said Out Loud.

Edmund Ross teaches the series' final lesson in advance. When the whole room demands a conviction, the rarest and most Christlike act available to a human being is to stand in the well of the court and decline — because the case hasn't been proven, because the file is missing pages, because there is an Advocate who has already answered it. Kennedy called that courage. Scripture goes further and gives it a family name: the sons of God are known for making peace, and their Elder Brother is known for one occupation above all the others.

He always lives to make intercession.

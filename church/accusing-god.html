Accusing God — God in the Dock

Part of Litigating Without a License. Watson named the second direction of accusation: "as he sometimes accuses us to God — so he accuses God to us" (Diabolos — The Accuser). This page follows that direction down: every unlicensed courtroom, left running long enough, eventually calls God to the stand. And most of us file our charges against heaven quietly — as worry, anxiety, and fear.

Lewis: the reversed courtroom

"The ancient man approached God (or even the gods) as the accused person approaches his judge. For the modern man the roles are reversed. He is the judge: God is in the dock. He is quite a kindly judge: if God should have a reasonable defence for being the god who permits war, poverty and disease, he is ready to listen to it. The trial may even end in God's acquittal. But the important thing is that Man is on the Bench and God in the Dock." — C.S. Lewis, "God in the Dock" (1948)

Lewis is describing modernity, but the arraignment is older than modernity — it is the oldest case on file.

The original indictment — Eden

The first slander in Scripture is a case against God, and it runs exactly on the working definition — a verdict defensible with the data but not the truth:

  • Gen 3:1–5 — the data: one prohibited tree. The serpent's verdict: "God knows that when you eat of it... you will be like God" — He is withholding; His command is self-interested; His threat is a bluff ("you will not surely die"). Defensible with the visible data; false about the Giver of every other tree
  • Gen 3:12 — and the first recorded human words after the fall are a counter-suit: "The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me fruit" — Adam deflects his own arraignment by prosecuting God's providence

"Bringing charges against God starts in Eden. Do you remember what happens after sin has been committed?... The woman you gave me, God — she tempted me. Implication: if you'd given me a better woman, this wouldn't have happened." — D.A. Carson, Call & Burden of Jeremiah

  • Gen 3:10 — and note what follows the first believed slander of God: "I was afraid... and I hid myself." Fear of God-as-hard-master enters the world in the same hour as the accusation. Keep that sequence; it returns below

The wilderness docket — the Numbers case

The link from Slander, Gossip, and the Busybody: the spies' dibbah (Num 13:32) looked like a report about terrain. Within one night it matured into a formal indictment of God:

  • Num 14:2–3 — "Would that we had died in Egypt!... Why is the LORD bringing us into this land, to fall by the sword?" — the evil report about circumstances was always a draft indictment of providence
  • Deut 1:27 — Moses quotes the charge in full: "you murmured in your tents and said, 'Because the LORD hated us he has brought us out of the land of Egypt, to give us into the hand of the Amorites, to destroy us.'" There is the verdict under all grumbling: He hates us. Data: Amorites, walls, giants. Truth: eagle's wings (Exod 19:4)
  • Ps 78:19–22 — the pattern named as courtroom speech: "They spoke against God, saying, 'Can God spread a table in the wilderness?'... because they did not believe in God and did not trust his saving power." Asaph classifies unbelief's inner monologue as testimony against God
  • Exod 16:8 — the venue ruling: "Your grumbling is not against us but against the LORD." All murmuring about circumstance is misaddressed litigation — the complaint department is the throne
  • Num 21:5; 1 Cor 10:9–10 — "spoke against God and against Moses"; Paul serves the warning on the church: "nor grumble, as some of them did"
  • The discovery fraud underneath the whole docket is ingratitude — the deleted exhibits: the Red Sea was weeks old when the first indictment was filed (Ps 106:13, "they soon forgot his works"). No suit against providence survives an honest benefits ledger

Malachi — the courtroom book

A whole prophetic book structured as cross-filed lawsuits: God brings charges through his prosecutor, and the people counter-sue — "How have you loved us?" (1:2); "'Everyone who does evil is good in the sight of the LORD'... 'Where is the God of justice?'" (2:17); "Your words have been hard against me... You have said, 'It is vain to serve God'" (3:13–14).

"Malachi is acting like a prosecuting attorney... These are the things you're doing wrong: You're not trusting that the Lord loves you... You're accusing God of being unjust." — Chris Oswald, Renewing Giving (Malachi series)

And note who is exempted from the docket: "then those who feared the LORD spoke with one another... and a book of remembrance was written" (3:16) — the counter-community is marked by right fear and different speech.

Job — the case that would not file

The accuser's real target in Job 1–2 was never Job's property; it was a filing: "he will curse you to your face." Satan's win condition is getting the saint to indict God.

  • Job 1:22 — "In all this Job did not sin or charge God with wrong" — the non-filing is the victory ("If the grace of God prevents our suffering from causing us to sin, then Satan is defeated... Satan's hope is to make us curse God, to doubt God's goodness" — Chris Oswald, The Steadfastness of Job)
  • Job 40:8 — when Job's protests drift toward the bench, God names the maneuver exactly: "Will you even put me in the wrong? Will you condemn me that you may be in the right?" — the dock, verbatim, two and a half millennia before Lewis. Self-justification always needs a defendant, and God is the defendant of last resort (cf. Rom 9:20; Rom 3:4 — "Let God be true though every one were a liar")

Worry and anxiety: the quiet indictment

Here is where the page lands on ordinary Tuesday-afternoon life. Worry rarely says "God is negligent." It just deliberates as if the verdict were in. Anxiety is the silent tribunal with God as the defendant — the functional ruling: He will not come through for me. Defensible with the visible data (the bank balance, the diagnosis, the empty pantry of the wilderness); false against the full record.

  • Matt 6:25–34 — notice that Jesus fights anxiety with data: birds fed, lilies clothed, "are you not of more value than they?" The anxious heart has not read too much evidence but too little — and cherry-picked the exhibits. His verdict on the anxious verdict: "O you of little faith" — and his diagnosis of its pedigree: "the Gentiles seek after all these things" — anxiety runs on functional orphanhood, "your heavenly Father knows"
  • 1 Pet 5:7 — "casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" — the ground clause is the point: anxiety retained is the suspicion, held in the body, that he doesn't
  • Rom 8:32 — the exhibit that closes the case: "He who did not spare his own Son... how will he not also with him graciously give us all things?" The cross is entered into evidence against every draft indictment of providence
  • Phil 4:6–7 — the alternative venue: "in everything by prayer... let your requests be made known to God" — anxiety re-filed as petition — venue transfer

"Fear. Unbelief. The nice word we use for it: anxiety. In so many ways, they're the antithesis of faith." — Chris Oswald, only-believe (on "Do not fear, only believe," Mark 5:36)

"You know who really worries about what they'll eat and drink?... Jesus says unbelievers, the nations, the Gentiles... Why is your faith so small? 'O ye of little faith.'" — Chris Oswald, Do Not Be Anxious

"Anxiety is silenced when the nearness of God is recalled. Doubts diminish when they encounter the living and active Word." — Chris Oswald, Grace and Peace

Fear: the accusation believed

Fear is what the body does with a slanderous portrait of God once the heart has accepted it.

  • Matt 25:24–25 — the one-talent servant is the proof text: "I knew you to be a hard man... so I was afraid, and I went and hid your talent." The fear flows from the verdict — and the verdict is Watson's line verbatim: the devil "accuses God to us, and says, 'He is a hard Master.'" The servant's paralysis, buried gifts, and risk-aversion are the fruit of a believed accusation. (Note the master's reply turns the servant's own portrait into his sentence — the measure-principle of The Speck and the Log applied to slandering God)
  • Gen 3:10 — the sequence again: accusation believed → "I was afraid" → hiding. Fear of God-as-hard-master is Eden's oldest symptom
  • Prov 29:25 — "the fear of man lays a snare, but whoever trusts in the LORD is safe" — every fear is a trust-statement about something
  • 1 Pet 3:5–6 — the bridge to the wiki's next page: "the holy women who hoped in God" adorned themselves with the imperishable quiet spirit — "and you are her children, if you do good and do not fear anything that is frightening." Peter sets hope in God and fear as the two possible engines of a woman's inner life — and by extension, of the contentious, controlling, managing spirit that fear produces. The eshet midyanim and the fearful woman are often the same woman: contention is frequently fear executing its verdict about God through management of people. (Developed at Eshet Midyanim)

The licensed complaint: lament is not accusation

The Bible does not silence the sufferer — it gives him a venue. Fully a third of the Psalter is complaint. The distinction that keeps lament licensed:

  • Jer 12:1 — the model filing, and it uses riv: "You are always righteous, LORD, when I bring a case (riv) before you — yet I would plead my case: why does the way of the wicked prosper?" Jeremiah affirms the Judge's righteousness in the act of filing the complaint. Lament argues with God from within trust; accusation passes a verdict on God from outside it
  • Hab 1:2–4 → 3:17–18 — the book that opens with riv and madon in the streets closes "yet I will rejoice in the LORD" — the complaint prayed all the way through comes out as worship
  • Ps 88 — permitted to end in darkness: "these aren't accusations... a recognition of God's sovereign control. He's asking God to stop, but in that sovereignty you hear a deep yearning for hope" (Chris Oswald, The Dark Night of the Soul)
  • Exod 5:22 — even a defective complaint filed at the right bench beats a polished verdict passed in the tent: "This is far from a perfect prayer — he's accusing God of sin... but at least it's a prayer. Moses turned to the Lord" (Chris Oswald, Leadership and the Crisis of Confidence, 2024-06-16). Contrast Num 14: same genre of grievance, but murmured in the tents, about God, not at the throne, to him. The address on the envelope is the difference between lament and mutiny
  • Jude 22 — "have mercy on those who doubt" — doubt brought into the light is a pastoral category, not a crime: "the temptation to doubt the trustworthiness of God... is common to all Christians. No Christian is exempt" (C.J. Mahaney, When Someone Doubts, drawing on Os Guinness, God in the Dark — the fitting companion title to Lewis's God in the Dock: doubt in the dark seeks God's face; accusation in the dock demands his defense)

The dock accepted

The end of this page is the gospel's strangest turn: God consented to stand in the dock. At the trial of Jesus the reversed courtroom of Eden reached its logical terminus — creature on the bench, Creator arraigned, false witnesses suborned, the innocent verdict-ed guilty. And he submitted to it — "he did not revile in return... but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" (1 Pet 2:23) — and the false conviction became the true acquittal of the accusers (2 Cor 5:21). Rom 3:25–26: the cross is where God answered the standing charge against his justice — "to show his righteousness... that he might be just and the justifier." The only defendant who ever stood in the dock innocent turned the dock into the mercy seat. Every remaining indictment of God is therefore not just false — it is late. The case was answered at Calvary, and the answer was not an argument but a substitution (Rom 8:32, the standing exhibit).

The page in one line

Grumbling, worry, anxiety, and fear are the quiet paperwork of a suit against heaven — a dibbah about providence, defensible with the visible data, false against the cross; lament files the same grief at the right bench, and faith reads the full record: "he who did not spare his own Son."

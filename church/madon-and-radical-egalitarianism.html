Madon and Radical Egalitarianism — Disputing the Disbursement of Power

The fourth application page of Litigating Without a License. Radical egalitarianism — the demand that all disparities of station, authority, and honor be read as injustices to be leveled — is, in this wiki's vocabulary, a standing dispute with God's providential disbursement of power. Scripture teaches both halves of what the leveler tears apart: ontological equality of soul (every man an image-bearer) and complementarity of station within the systems God has created — church, government, family. The rage against the second half is never really aimed at Moses, the magistrate, or the husband; it is a suit against the Disburser — and it is as old as a snake promising equality with God to a woman who already had everything else.

The original leveling project

  • Gen 3:5 — the first egalitarian pitch: "you will be like God." Note what the serpent does not promise — food, wealth, pleasure. He promises parity of station. The first sin is not appetite; it is an objection to the org chart of Eden
  • Isa 14:13–14 — the pitch-man's own portfolio: "I will ascend above the heights of the clouds; I will make myself like the Most High." The accuser's original project is upward leveling — and every subsequent leveling movement he sponsors is franchise work
  • Luke 4:5–7 — and note his standing claim: showing Jesus the kingdoms, "to you I will give all this authority... for it has been delivered to me, and I give it to whom I will." Satan asserts disbursement rights — a counterfeit Ps 75:7. The counterfeit kingdom's whole platform is a rival theory of who allocates power ("If you will worship me, it will all be yours"). The devil is not an anarchist; he is a rival administrator — his kingdom apes God's in every structural respect except its head and its currency, and his recruiting pitch, from Eden forward, is equality — while the product delivered is always tyranny (John 8:34, 44)

The disbursement doctrine

Scripture is unembarrassed and explicit: station is allocated from the bench.

  • Ps 75:6–7 — the anchor: "For not from the east or from the west and not from the wilderness comes lifting up, but it is God who executes judgment, putting down one and lifting up another." Promotion is a judicial act. To dispute the disbursement is to appeal the Judge
  • Dan 2:21; 4:17 — "he removes kings and sets up kings"; "the Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will, and sets over it the lowliest of men" — stated twice to the most powerful man alive, the second time on his way to eat grass
  • Rom 13:1 — "there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God"
  • John 19:11 — Jesus affirms the doctrine to Pilate, about Pilate, mid-miscarriage-of-justice: "You would have no authority over me at all unless it had been given you from above"
  • 1 Cor 12:18 — and inside the church: "God arranged the members in the body, each one of them, as he chose"

Equal souls, differentiated stations

The Bible's actual position, both planks at full strength:

Ontological equality: Gen 1:27 (every human the imago Dei); Job 31:13–15 — Job's own doctrine, stated about his servants: "Did not he who made me in the womb make him?"; Prov 22:2 — "the rich and the poor meet together; the LORD is the maker of them all"; Acts 17:26; Gal 3:28 (one standing in Christ — a verse about justification's equality, routinely conscripted to erase the stations Paul goes on writing about in the same letters); 1 Pet 3:7 (co-heirs).

Complementarity of station within God's created systems — church (Heb 13:17; 1 Tim 3; 1 Cor 12), government (Rom 13:1–7; 1 Pet 2:13–17), family (Eph 5:22–6:9; 1 Pet 3:1–7) — with 1 Cor 12:14–26 as the both/and in one passage: many members, differing functions and honors, no member's standing diminished ("the eye cannot say to the hand, 'I have no need of you'"), the lesser parts clothed with greater honor, and the whole arrangement "as he chose." From the corpus:

"Any institution God has created will have a hierarchy, because he's patterning them off of his order within the Godhead. And every institution that has an authority structure will sooner or later be hijacked by sinful people who want to abuse that authority structure." — Chris Oswald, The Power to Submit (2017-11-12)

"We live in an aggressively egalitarian and individualized age, and we recoil at the idea that someone else's actions could determine our destiny — but that's just the way it is... a self-evident truth that God endorses as he talks about who we are throughout Scripture." — Chris Oswald, The Final Adam (2025-12-07, on headship)

The Westminster Larger Catechism: the fifth commandment as the anti-madon charter

The Reformed tradition built the full architecture under one commandment. The WLC reads "honor thy father and thy mother" as governing every station relation — and its vice lists are this wiki's vocabulary in seventeenth-century dress:

  • Q124 — the scope: "By father and mother... are meant, not only natural parents, but all superiors in age and gifts; and especially such as, by God's ordinance, are over us in place of authority, whether in family, church, or commonwealth" — the three systems, named
  • Q127 — duties to superiors include "bearing with their infirmities, and covering them in love" — Prov 10:12's covering, applied up the chain (the exact opposite of Ham's move, and of the exposé instinct)
  • Q128 — the sins of inferiors: "envying at, contempt of, and rebellion against, them and their places" — envy at the place itself: the catechism knows that the quarrel is with the disbursement, not the officeholder
  • Q130 — and the balance the levelers say the tradition lacks: the sins of superiors get their own long answer — "an inordinate seeking of themselves, their own glory... commanding things unlawful... careless exposing, or leaving them to wrong, temptation, and danger... dishonoring themselves, or lessening their authority, by an unjust, indiscreet, rigorous, or remiss behavior." Authority abused is prosecuted too — in the licensed court, under the same commandment
  • Q131–132 — the masterpiece, on equals: the duty is "to regard the dignity and worth of each other, in giving honor to go one before another; and to rejoice in each other's gifts and advancement, as their own" — joy at a rival's increase, catechized. And the sins of equals: "the undervaluing of the worth, envying the gifts, grieving at the advancement or prosperity one of another; and usurping pre-eminence one over another" — grieving at advancement: the catechism indicts the emotion itself, the inward wince at a peer's promotion, as a fifth-commandment violation. The silent tribunal's docket includes the org chart

The gallery of levelers

  • Miriam and Aaron (Num 12:2) — the family version: "Has the LORD indeed spoken only through Moses? Has he not spoken through us also?" — a true premise (he had, 12:2 is not wrong) pressed into a leveling claim; God's answer distinguishes stations among prophets (12:6–8: "not so with my servant Moses... with him I speak mouth to mouth") and the accuser of the higher station leaves the tent leprous — until Moses intercedes for her
  • Korah (Num 16:3) — the ecclesial version, the egalitarian argument par excellence: "All in the congregation are holy, every one of them, and the LORD is among them. Why then do you exalt yourselves above the assembly of the LORD?" — a data-defensible premise (Exod 19:6 says they are a holy nation) welded to a false verdict about disbursement. Moses' reply aims at the engine: "is it too small a thing for you that the God of Israel has separated you... to do service in the tabernacle?... would you seek the priesthood also?" (16:9–10) — Korah already held an allocated honor; the dispute was never about equality but about his elevation. Radical egalitarianism is routinely ambition wearing equality's clothes
  • Dathan and Abiram (Num 16:13) — the political version, with the accusation inverted for maximum audacity: "you have brought us up out of a land flowing with milk and honey" — Egypt re-audited as the promised land (rigging the record at maximum) — "that you may make yourself a prince over us"
  • Absalom (2 Sam 15:1–6) — the demagogue's full playbook, and the page's flagship. Watch the three moves: (1) harvest the grievances: he stations himself beside the way of the gate and intercepts every man "who had a dispute (riv) to come to the king for judgment" — the coup runs through the courts; the leveler recruits among litigants; (2) slander the administration and promise better verdicts: "your claims are good and right, but there is no man designated by the king to hear you. Oh that I were judge in the land! Then every man with a dispute... might come to me, and I would give him justice" — a dibbah on David's bench plus a campaign promise of unlimited din; (3) the performed humility: "when a man came near to pay homage to him, he would put out his hand and take hold of him and kiss him" — he refuses the genuflection, plays just-one-of-you, the prince who won't be bowed to — and the narrator names the transaction: "so Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel." The egalitarian gesture was acquisition. He declined the bow in order to collect the man; the refused homage was the price of the whole kingdom. (Absalom is Satan's politics in miniature: the leveling pitch in service of a counterfeit throne — he even ends hanged on a tree between heaven and earth.) And note where David spends the coup: Psalms 3–4, sleeping — "I lay down and slept; I woke again, for the LORD sustained me" (3:5) — the deposed king hands the case up and sleeps, while the heart-thief campaigns
  • The disciples (Luke 22:24) — lest the gallery feel safely historical: "a dispute (philoneikia — love of rivalry) arose among them, as to which of them was to be regarded as the greatest" — at the Last Supper table, minutes after the bread and cup
  • The envy of Jesus' popularity — the Pharisees at the parade: "You see that you are gaining nothing. Look, the whole world has gone after him" (John 12:19) — the allocation of the crowd's hearts read as a zero-sum injury, ripening in days into "it was out of envy that they delivered him up" (Matt 27:18). The greatest crime in history was, at the human level, a dispute over the disbursement of glory
  • James's assemblies (James 2:1–9) — favoritism (prosōpolēmpsia) as the same jockeying from below: seating the gold-ringed man well is an attempted alliance with station — courting power instead of storming it, but the same worship of the org chart, and James files it as "have you not... become judges with evil thoughts?" — the usher's chair becomes a corrupt bench

Subsection: Madon and Critical Theory

Critical theory is radical egalitarianism systematized into a hermeneutic of accusation — a total interpretive method in which every disparity of outcome or station is read as evidence of oppression. Run it through the wiki's instruments:

  1. It is a verdict machine built on the definition of slander. The data are real — disparities exist, and some genuinely are the fruit of oppression (Scripture prosecutes real oppression relentlessly: Isa 1:17; 10:1–2; Amos throughout; James 5:1–6). But the method converts every disparity into one verdict by axiom — defensible with the data, but not the truth, because disparity has many fathers (providence's differing gifts, Matt 25:15's "to each according to his ability," sowing and reaping, and yes, sometimes sin). A hermeneutic that permits only the oppression-verdict is the epistemology of pride institutionalized: all the data, one interpretation, no cross-examination (Prov 18:17 abolished as a discipline)
  2. "Conflict theory" is society-as-madon stated as a first principle. Where Scripture sees created systems capable of shalom and corruptible by sin, the critical frame defines every relation — sexes, classes, races, generations — as a power struggle. It is zero-sum theology applied to authority and honor: a fixed pie of power, so every station is stolen goods. The oppressor/oppressed binary then assigns permanent courtroom roles by group: a class of standing accusers and a class of standing defendants — guilt heritable, individual evidence inadmissible (against Deut 24:16 and Ezek 18), acquittal structurally impossible
  3. It is a courtroom with no advocate and no closed docket. No atonement, no forgiveness mechanism, no statute of limitations, no verdict that ends the case — grievance without terminus, which is why its politics feel like Rev 12:10's job description: accusation day and night. It inverts Lev 19:15 — the one verse that forbids partiality in both directions ("You shall not be partial to the poor or defer to the great, but in righteousness shall you judge your neighbor") — into a jurisprudence of mandated partiality with reversed polarity. And in Girardian terms (Madon and Mimesis), it does not abolish scapegoating; it re-aims it — the mimetic crisis discharged upward onto the "oppressor" class is still the accuser's mechanism, with new defendants and the same unanimity
  4. What it gets right is what the Bible got right first — and does better. Real power abuses real people; the wiki's own doctrine is that licensed din runs downhill, that God "executes justice for the oppressed" (Ps 146:7), and that the sins of superiors get their own catechism answer (WLC Q130). The difference is total, though: biblical justice tries cases (individual, evidenced, cross-examined, both-ways impartial) before a Judge who can close dockets — critical theory tries categories, forever. One ends in shalom; the other is a perpetual-motion madon engine, and its fuel is the same envy Q132 catechizes against

The inverted ledger — and the strange patience of God. Critical theory is not a new logic; it is the inversion of the oldest bad logic on record — the ancient assumption that the prosperous are obviously favored by God and the suffering obviously judged. The doctrine of common grace is the Bible's answer to a problem the Bible keeps raising itself. The problem: the wicked seem to be doing fine — what is the deal with that? Scripture does not hide this question in an appendix. It raises it in Job, raises it in Psalm 73, raises it in Jeremiah 12, raises it in Qoheleth (Eccl 7:15; 8:14), and it is still on the table when the disciples open their mouths in John 9. They pass a man blind from birth and ask Jesus, "Rabbi, who sinned — this man or his parents — that he was born blind?"

Notice the machinery of that question. It assumes a flat, immediate, one-to-one moral accounting: those with good lives must be doing good things; those with bad lives must be doing bad things. Suffering is a receipt, and prosperity is a receipt, and you can read a man's account balance right off his circumstances. That is how the human heart calculates by default. It is how Job's friends calculated. It is how the disciples calculated — men who had walked with Jesus and still ran the old math.

And our generation has not outgrown this instinct. It has merely inverted it. The old error said: the people on top must be righteous, and the people on the bottom must deserve it. The reigning modern framework — critical theory in its various forms — simply flips the ledger: the people on top must be oppressors, and the people on the bottom must be innocent. Position still equals verdict; only the sign has changed. And the inversion is, if anything, less useful than the original error, because at least the old assumption occasionally correlated with sowing and reaping. The new one reads every asymmetry as an indictment and every success as a theft. Both systems make the same mistake Qoheleth refuses to make: they let "I see" write the whole story (the epistemology of pride, run on circumstances). Neither has any room for the strange patience of God.

Jesus breaks the machinery in both directions with the same hammer: to the disciples' old math — "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him" (John 9:3); to the crowd reading the tower of Siloam's casualty list as a verdict roster — "do you think that they were worse offenders than all the others?... No, I tell you" (Luke 13:2–5). Circumstances are not receipts. Prosperity on the wicked is common grace — "he makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good, and sends rain on the just and on the unjust" (Matt 5:45; Acts 14:17) — and common grace is not acquittal but patience: "God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance" (Rom 2:4; 2 Pet 3:9). Suffering on the righteous is not a verdict but a venue for glory (John 9:3) or a Job file still under seal. Both ledger-readings — ancient and critical — are data-defensible verdicts at civilizational scale, and both are exposed at the same address: the most prosperous-looking verdict-reversal in history is a crucified man the whole crowd's ledger read as "stricken, smitten by God" (Isa 53:4) — position equaling verdict, wrong by the width of the gospel

The true order: how God levels, and how greatness descends

The Bible has its own revolution, and its direction is the tell:

  • God does level — downward from the bench, in his own time. "He has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate" (Luke 1:52; cf. 1 Sam 2:7–8 — Hannah sang it first: "the LORD makes poor and makes rich; he brings low and he exalts; he raises up the poor from the dust... to make them sit with princes"). The Magnificat is not Korah's platform; it is Ps 75:7 in the future tense — leveling as God's judicial act, not the mob's acquisition. Hence the standing instruction: "humble yourselves under the mighty hand of God, that at the proper time he may exalt you" (1 Pet 5:6) — promotion sought at the bench, not seized at the gate
  • And true greatness moves the opposite direction from Absalom. "The kings of the Gentiles lord it over them... but not so with you. Rather, let the greatest among you become as the youngest, and the leader as one who serves. I am among you as the one who serves" (Luke 22:25–27 — spoken into the disciples' greatness-dispute). The One actually entitled to every genuflection washed the feet (John 13); the One for whom equality with God was fact "did not count it a thing to be grasped" (Phil 2:6 — the anti-Eden, the anti-Isa-14). Absalom performed lowliness to take a throne; Christ performed lowliness because he owned one — the kiss at the gate versus the towel at the table. And the licensed competition is honor's only lawful race: "outdo one another in showing honor" (Rom 12:10) — the single arena where Scripture commands you to beat your neighbor
  • Jesus' relationship to the structures he lived under settles the posture: "Jesus was not a nonconformist. He resisted the world's structures because he was a hyperconformist to the Father" (Chris Oswald, Seeing Christ in the Psalms, Part 1, 2025-06-01). Submission to God's disbursement is not servility; it is the only position from which power can be held, or lacked, without madon

The page in one line

Radical egalitarianism is Korah's syllogism running on Eden's engine — true premise (all are holy), false verdict (therefore no stations), real target (the Disburser) — perfected by Absalom's kiss and systematized by critical theory into a courtroom that never closes; against it stands a Bible that makes every soul equal, every station assigned, every abuse of station triable, every promotion God's judicial act, and every greatness a towel — in a kingdom whose King refused the leveler's shortcut because all that is his Father's was already his.

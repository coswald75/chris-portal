Madon and Mimesis — Borrowed Desire, Rigged Ledgers, and the Only Safe Model

The third application page of Litigating Without a License. René Girard's thesis: human desire is mimetic — we do not desire things directly; we desire what we see others desiring, borrow our wants from models, and therefore converge on the same objects, which turns models into rivals and communities into courtrooms. Scripture said it first, twice, bluntly: "Then I saw that all toil and all skill in work come from a man's envy of his neighbor" (Eccl 4:4), and "What causes quarrels (machai) and fights among you?... You desire and do not have, so you murder. You covet and cannot obtain, so you fight and quarrel" (James 4:1–2). Mimetic desire is madon's supply chain — and its hidden premise is a lie about God's economy.

The diagnosis: you are a mimetic figure

From the sermon this page is built on — Narcan for the Soul (Ecclesiastes series), on Eccl 4:4:

"People spend their whole lives trying to get stuff just because their neighbor has that stuff. This is mimetic desire on display... Most people are pursuing life goals that were more caught than taught, more absorbed than intentionally formulated."

"Why is that car the one you'd be a little proud to park in the driveway? And who taught you to be proud of it? Where did you get your sense of what a successful 30-year-old looks like?... Have you ever had a single original desire? Are you sure?"

"Here's where most people will lie to you. They will suggest that you are capable of coming up with your own — and you're not. You are a mimetic figure... It's a closed system. We are going to value things by looking at someone else."

The honesty here is the page's foundation, and it is where the sermon out-runs most of Girard's popularizers: the exits are fake. You will not reason your way to original desire from first principles; the advertising industry knows this about you even if you don't ("have you even been manly enough to consider the possibility that people are trying to manipulate you and yours?"). Comparison is the native motion of the heart — "we go onto Instagram and behold what other people have... transformed to be coveters, to be professional comparers" (Chris Oswald, When God Gives Us What We Demand, 2018-08-26). The question is never whether you will imitate; it is whom.

Girard's arc ends at this wiki's front door

Follow mimetic desire downstream and you arrive at the accuser — Girard himself made the trip:

  1. Borrowed desire converges rivals on one object (two hands, one prize)
  2. Rivalry turns the model into the obstacle — the one who taught you to want it now stands between you and it (Saul hands David the songs, then "eyed David from that day on," 1 Sam 18:9 — threatened glory is mimetic rivalry named)
  3. Contagion — desire and hostility both herd (the anxious system and the envious system run on the same crowd physics)
  4. The scapegoat — the community discharges its mimetic crisis by uniting in accusation against a victim: "it is better for you that one man should die for the people" (Caiaphas, John 11:50 — the mechanism stated as policy). Girard's late work drew the conclusion this wiki is built on: Satan is the accusation principle itself — the prosecutor who organizes crowds around victims — and his opposite number is the Paraclete, the counsel for the defense (I See Satan Fall Like Lightning; see Diabolos — The Accuser and The Advocate (Paraklētos)). The Gospels, Girard argued, are the texts that finally expose the mechanism, because they insist — against every myth — that the victim is innocent. The crucifixion is the scapegoat mechanism run on the one Man who breaks it: convicted by unanimous mimetic accusation ("out of envy they delivered him up," Matt 27:18 — Pilate could see the engine), and vindicated by resurrection, "disarming the rulers and authorities and putting them to open shame" (Col 2:15)

The Ten Commandments already knew all of it: the Decalogue ends with the anti-mimetic law — "You shall not covet your neighbor's house... your neighbor's wife... or anything that is your neighbor's" (Exod 20:17). Every forbidden object is defined by whose it is. The tenth commandment does not ban desire; it bans borrowed desire — desire routed through the neighbor — because God knew where it ends: the ninth commandment (false witness) sits next to the tenth for supply-chain reasons.

The hidden premise: zero-sum theology

Here is the lie underneath the whole mimetic machine: the assumption that reality is a fixed pie — that glory, goods, and blessing exist in constant supply, so that your neighbor's portion diminishes yours, and every gain must be seized from someone. Call it zero-sum theology, and notice that it is a doctrine of God before it is an economics: covetousness is an objection to God's provisioning; envy is an objection to God's allocation of glory (the quarrel with God's allocation). Both charge the Provisioner — either he cannot make more, or he deals it unjustly. Envy never merely wants the neighbor's thing; it disputes the Giver's ledger.

The secular versions make the theology visible. Malthus (Essay on the Principle of Population, 1798) is zero-sum theology systematized: population outruns food, the pie is fixed, scarcity is destiny, and every extra person at the table is a threat — a framework that has been empirically embarrassed for two centuries (innovation keeps enlarging the pie Malthus declared fixed) yet keeps resurrecting, because it flatters the envious heart's math. George Gilder (Knowledge and Power, 2013) states the refutation in information-theoretic terms: wealth is not a hoard of stuff to be divided but knowledge; growth is learning; the economy is not a fixed-sum incentive machine but an information system in which entrepreneurial surprise creates what did not exist. The materialist superstition — that wealth is a static pile someone else is hogging — is, in Gilder's account, precisely what mimetic politics and mimetic envy run on. Creation is generative because it is spoken by a generative God: "be fruitful and multiply" (Gen 1:28) is a command with no fixed pie in it.

And Scripture's economy is explicitly non-zero-sum wherever you test it:

  • Ps 50:10–12 — "every beast of the forest is mine, the cattle on a thousand hills... If I were hungry, I would not tell you, for the world and its fullness are mine" — the Provisioner is not rationing
  • Prov 11:24 — "One gives freely, yet grows all the richer; another withholds what he should give, and only suffers want" — the ledger runs backward from Malthus; 2 Cor 9:8–11 — "God is able to make all grace abound to you, so that having all sufficiency in all things at all times, you may abound in every good work... enriched in every way to be generous"
  • The loaves — five loaves, five thousand fed, twelve baskets over: the Lord's arithmetic demonstrated on bread; the talents — the master's economy rewards venture and multiplies the entrusted sum, and the only loser is the zero-sum servant (accusation with a shovel — the buried talent is Malthusian theology in a hole)
  • Luke 15:31 — the elder brother runs a zero-sum audit of the father's house ("you never gave me a young goat" — while the fattened calf goes to the rival), and the father refutes the entire ledger in one sentence: "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours." Nothing was subtracted from him by the feast. Envy had rigged his record (deleted exhibits: always with me had been entered at zero)
  • John 3:26–30 — the anti-mimetic man in full: John's disciples sound the rivalry alarm ("he is baptizing, and all are going to him"), and John answers with the whole page in three sentences: "A person cannot receive even one thing unless it is given him from heaven" (allocation belongs to God — the quarrel is with him or with no one); "He must increase, but I must decrease" (the rival's increase accepted as the plan); "therefore this joy of mine is now complete" — joy at a rival's increase, the rarest sound on earth and the definitive tell of a settled account. The Baptist is what a human being looks like with the mimetic engine unplugged
  • Ps 23:5 — and the table image that holds it all: "You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies... my cup overflows." Abundance served in the middle of rivalry — the overflowing cup as the practical refutation of the fixed pie

James closes the loop himself: after "you covet and cannot obtain, so you fight," the diagnosis lands not on scarcity but on venue — "You do not have, because you do not ask" (James 4:2). The mimetic fighter grabs at the neighbor because he has never filed a request with the infinite Giver (the bench is open); and when he does ask, he asks mimetically — "to spend it on your passions" (4:3). Petition aimed at the Provisioner is the anti-Malthusian act: it takes the ledger dispute to the only party who can print more.

Comparison, the thief of joy: "a king like the nations"

The proverb commonly credited to Roosevelt — comparison is the thief of joy — is wiki-precise: joy is the evidence of a settled account, and comparison is the act that re-opens the ledger. Ps 4:7 is built on the contrast: "more joy in my heart than they have when their grain and wine abound" — the one comparison that enriches is the one that ends at God's sufficiency. Every other comparison files a deficiency claim. And Israel ran the national-scale experiment: 1 Samuel 8.

  • The demand is mimetic to the letter. "Appoint for us a king to judge us like all the nations" (8:5); "that we also may be like all the nations, and that our king may go out before us and fight our battles" (8:20). Not a king — a king like theirs. The specification is borrowed; the desire is routed entirely through the neighbor-nations. And the timing convicts them of rigging the record: the demand comes one chapter after Mizpah, where God routed the Philistines with thunder and Samuel raised the Ebenezer — "Till now the LORD has helped us" (7:12). The stone was still new when "who will fight our battles?" got filed. Deleted exhibit, standing in the field
  • God had already provided for the desire — un-mimetically. Deut 17:14–20 anticipates the very request ("I will set a king over me, like all the nations") and grants kingship on God's terms: a king whom the LORD chooses, a brother ("one from among your brothers... his heart may not be lifted up above his brothers"), constitutionally restrained — no multiplied horses, wives, or gold, and a hand-copied Torah he reads all his life. A non-extractive, non-taking, brother-king. The sin at Ramah was not wanting a king; it was wanting the nations' model instead of God's — comparison-informed covetousness, which God himself files as accusation: "they have not rejected you, but they have rejected me from being king over them" (8:7). The demand for the neighbor's king was a verdict on God's kingship — a suit against providence alleging under-provision, filed against the Provider with the Ebenezer in view
  • Samuel reads them the invoice. The warning of 8:11–18 is one verb conjugated: he will take. Your sons for his chariots; your daughters for his kitchens; your fields, vineyards, and olive orchards — "the best of them"; a tenth of your grain and flocks; your servants and your donkeys — "and you shall be his slaves. And in that day you will cry out because of your king, whom you have chosen for yourselves, but the LORD will not answer you in that day." The nations' king is a taking-king by design; that is what "like the nations" means
  • The invoice was paid in full, with interest, in civil war. Solomon's splendor ran on the warned machinery — conscripted labor, requisition districts, the heavy yoke — until the nation stood at Shechem begging his son: "Your father made our yoke heavy. Lighten it" (1 Kgs 12:4). Rehoboam's answer is the taking-king perfected ("my little finger is thicker than my father's thighs... I will discipline you with scorpions"), and the kingdom split on the spot — Jeroboam, schism, and two royal lines whose catalog of failures fills Kings. The mimetic demand of 1 Samuel 8 is the direct ancestor of the civil war of 1 Kings 12: comparison-informed covetousness, accusing God of not providing, purchased the exact oppression God had offered to spare them — and the joy went where comparison always sends it. They had a warrior-King who charged nothing and answered when they cried (Judg 3:9; 1 Sam 7:9–10); they traded him for one who took everything and left them crying unanswered (8:18). That is the thief of joy's standard exchange rate

The solution: not original desire, but a better Model

The sermon's endgame, which is more honest than the self-help versions of Girard:

"You are going to value things by looking at someone else. And Jesus Christ knows that, and very much with genuine love in his heart tells you: Follow me. Imitate me. Look to me to set your determination of what's important and what's not... You're going to look to somebody. Look to Jesus." — Narcan for the Soul

Scripture never prescribes escaping mimesis — it harnesses it: "Be imitators (mimētai) of me, as I am of Christ" (1 Cor 11:1); "be imitators of God, as beloved children" (Eph 5:1); "you became imitators of us and of the Lord" (1 Thess 1:6); "consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith" (Heb 13:7). Discipleship is mimetic desire under new management. And Christ is the one Model with whom rivalry is structurally impossible — Girard's own conclusion, and Phil 2 shows why: the mind you are commanded to copy ("have this mind among yourselves") is the mind that "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped (harpagmos — seized), but emptied himself." Every other model teaches you to seize; imitating this model produces the one desire that cannot converge into rivalry, because what he wants for you is your good, and what he gives is not diminished by sharing — the Model is also the Bread, and five loaves feed five thousand. Imitate the neighbor and you will end at his throat or at his feet; imitate the Increaser and you end like the Baptist: decreasing, and full of joy.

The page in one line

Mimesis is madon's supply chain — borrowed desire converging rivals on a fixed pie that never existed, riding envy's quarrel with God's allocation all the way down to the scapegoat's cross — and the cure is not original desire (you have none) but the only safe Model: the God whose economy multiplies loaves, whose "all that is mine is yours" bankrupts the elder brother's audit, and whose friend John could watch the crowds change rivers and say my joy is now complete.

Sources: René Girard, esp. I See Satan Fall Like Lightning (1999); Thomas Malthus, An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798); George Gilder, Knowledge and Power (2013); Chris Oswald, Narcan for the Soul (Ecclesiastes series) and When God Gives Us What We Demand (2018-08-26).

The Internal Courtroom — The Silent Tribunal

Part of Litigating Without a License. Where Diabolos meets Madon: the heart convenes a court before the mouth says a word. The tribunal is silent — but it is fully in session, and Scripture treats it as jurisdiction God never granted.

The anatomy: madon is manufactured in the heart

  • Prov 6:14 — the worthless man: "with perverted heart devises evil, continually sowing contentions (midyanim)" — the direct lexical link. The madon that Proverbs pictures as fire and dripping is fabricated in the heart before it is sown
  • Prov 6:16–19 — the seven things the LORD hates run as a pipeline: "a heart that devises wicked plans, feet that make haste to run to evil, a false witness who breathes out lies, and one who sows discord (medanim) among brothers." Heart → feet → perjury → discord. The list climaxes with discord-sowing; it begins in the heart
  • Ps 140:1–2 — "men who plan evil things in their heart and stir up wars continually"
  • Zech 8:17 — "do not devise evil in your hearts against one another... for all these things I hate, declares the LORD"
  • Lev 19:15–18 — the foundational text (behind James, see below). One paragraph holds the whole system: the licensed court ("in righteousness shall you judge your neighbor," v15), the slander ban (v16, rakil), the heart ban ("you shall not hate your brother in your heart," v17), the licensed alternative ("you shall reason frankly with your neighbor, lest you incur sin," v17), and the crown (v18, "love your neighbor as yourself") — each sealed with "I am the LORD." Silent verdicts are forbidden by the same clause that commands open speech

Three case studies

1. Simon the Pharisee — Luke 7:36–50. "He said to himself (eipen en heautō), 'If this man were a prophet, he would have known who and what sort of woman this is'" (7:39). A two-count indictment held entirely in the head: the woman arraigned as sinner, Jesus arraigned as fraud. Jesus answers the unspoken case with an actual case — the two debtors, a juridical mashal like Nathan's (2 Sam 12) — and maneuvers Simon into rendering the verdict on himself ("Which of them will love him more?"). Then the cross-examination: "Do you see this woman?" — the evidence reviewed point by point (no water, no kiss, no oil), the ruling reversed, and the gallery reopening litigation aloud ("Who is this, who even forgives sins?").

2. The praying Pharisee — Luke 18:9–14. He "prayed thus with himself (pros heauton): 'God, I thank you that I am not like other men... or even like this tax collector.'" The silent tribunal wearing a robe of prayer — the indictment of a stranger smuggled into a thanksgiving. Note Luke's framing: told "to some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and treated others with contempt." Self-justification and other-condemnation are one motion; every internal verdict against a neighbor is simultaneously a verdict for the self. The tax collector, who files no case but his own ("God, be merciful to me, a sinner"), "went down to his house justified" — the court's actual ruling contradicts the tribunal's.

3. Michal — 2 Sam 6:16–23. She watched David dance "and she despised him in her heart." The verdict is reached at the window; the contemptuous speech comes later, at the door (6:20 — "How the king of Israel honored himself today!"). The internal madon → external contempt arc, in a marriage — the negative image of Sarah, whose honorable naming of her husband happened in her inner speech (Gen 18:12 ← 1 Pet 3:6; developed below).

Jesus hears the silent court

The Gospels show Jesus repeatedly cross-examining tribunals that never spoke:

  • Matt 9:3–4 — "some of the scribes said to themselves, 'This man is blaspheming.' But Jesus, knowing their thoughts, said, 'Why do you think evil in your hearts?'"
  • Mark 2:6–8 — "questioning in their hearts... Jesus, perceiving in his spirit that they thus questioned within themselves, said, 'Why do you question these things in your hearts?'"
  • Luke 6:8 — at the withered-hand trial: "he knew their thoughts"
  • Heb 4:12–13 — the word "discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart... no creature is hidden"; Jer 17:9–10 — "I the LORD search the heart"; 1 Sam 16:7 — "the LORD looks on the heart"

The silent tribunal's working assumption — no one can hear this — is false. There is always one auditor present, and he is the Judge whose seat is being borrowed.

Jesus' jurisprudence: the heart is arraigned in a real court

  • Matt 5:21–22 — heart-anger prosecuted in escalating venues: "everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment; whoever insults his brother will be liable to the council (synedrion); and whoever says 'You fool!' will be liable to the hell of fire." Jesus assigns courtrooms to interior states — the silent verdict is itself a triable offense
  • Matt 7:1–5 — "with the judgment you pronounce you will be judged" — the tribunal's own standard becomes its sentencing guideline; the log makes the critical eye a distorted instrument, disqualified as a witness
  • Luke 6:37–38 — "judge not... condemn not... forgive... the measure you use will be measured back to you"

The demonic pedigree

  • James 3:14–16 — "bitter jealousy and selfish ambition in your hearts... this wisdom is earthly, unspiritual, demonic (daimoniōdēs)." James assigns the internal critical disposition a demonic origin — before it ever speaks
  • James 4:1 — quarrels (machai) come from passions at war within you
  • James 4:11–12 — the judging brother "judges the law" and usurps the seat of "the one Lawgiver and Judge" — the tribunal is not merely unkind; it is a coup — the vacated prosecutor's office, staffed by volunteers

Jurisdiction and recusal

  • Rom 14:4 — "Who are you to pass judgment on the servant of another? It is before his own master that he stands or falls"
  • Rom 14:10–13 — "we will all stand before the judgment seat of God... decide (krinate — the same judging faculty, redirected!) never to put a stumbling block in the way of a brother." Paul doesn't amputate the judging faculty; he re-aims it at the self's conduct
  • 1 Cor 4:3–5 — the deepest recusal in Scripture: "I do not even judge myself... It is the Lord who judges me. Therefore do not pronounce judgment before the time." Even the self-directed tribunal must adjourn until the licensed Judge arrives — with a stunning docket note: at that court, "each one will receive his commendation from God"

The licensed alternative: open court

The silent tribunal's opposite is not silence about sin — it is open, licensed process:

  • Lev 19:17 — "you shall not hate your brother in your heart, but you shall reason frankly with your neighbor, lest you incur sin because of him." The frank word replaces the heart-verdict
  • Matt 18:15 — "go and tell him his fault, between you and him alone" — the case filed with the defendant present, not tried in absentia
  • Gal 6:1 — restore "in a spirit of gentleness. Keep watch on yourself" — the restorer under the same law as the restored

Adjournment: how the court closes

The tribunal cannot be argued down; it must be superseded. The gospel closes it from two directions:

  1. The docket against you is closed — Rom 8:33–34; Zech 3; Col 2:14. When the conscience accepts the verdict of the true court, the accuser loses standing in here as well as up there (see the Spiritual Warfare and the Psalms material in Diabolos — The Accuser: "He may still have standing in your courtroom if your conscience isn't fully standing on the gospel")
  2. The case against your neighbor is transferred — Ps 139:23–24, "Search me, O God, and know my heart" — the psalm that begins with God's total surveillance ends by handing him the gavel. And 1 Pet 2:23: Jesus, under real (not imagined) injustice, "did not revile in return... but continued entrusting himself to him who judges justly" — the case handed to the only licensed court

Inner speech: the tribunal's chamber, under new management

The silent tribunal runs on self-talk — every "said to himself" above is a man narrating a case to an audience of one. Scripture treats that private narration as fully moral action:

  • Deut 15:9 — "Take care lest there be an unworthy thought in your heart and you say, 'The seventh year, the year of release is near,' and your eye look grudgingly on your poor brother... and he cry to the LORD against you, and you be guilty of sin." A sentence spoken only inside the heart — private fiscal reasoning — is indicted by name, and the poor brother's counter-suit reaches the real court
  • Eph 5:33 with 1 Pet 3:6 ← Gen 18:12 — the marriage texts adjudicate here too. Respect is an interior verdict before it is ever a performed behavior: the honorable naming Peter commends in Sarah happened to herself, in her inner monologue — while Michal's contempt (above) was complete at the window before a word was spoken. Between those two women runs the whole difference between the tribunal and its replacement (see the fear-or-hope engine)

And the replacement is not silence. The Psalms model inner speech as advocacy — the saint takes over his own courtroom and conducts the defense's monologue instead of hosting the accuser's:

  • Ps 42:5, 11 — "Why are you cast down, O my soul?... Hope in God" — the psalmist stops listening to himself and starts preaching to himself: he cross-examines his own despair, then addresses it with the verdict
  • Ps 103:1–2 — "Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits" — commanded self-address, aimed exactly at the forgetting that lets the suit against providence get filed
  • Ps 116:7 — "Return, O my soul, to your rest; for the LORD has dealt bountifully with you" — the full record recited to the self
  • 2 Cor 10:5 — "take every thought captive to obey Christ" — the courtroom stays open under new management: the thoughts become the defendants, not the neighbors
  • Phil 4:8 — "whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just... think about these things" — an admissibility standard for the inner court's evidence

From the Hall

Citations by sermon title and date, as preserved in the corpus.

Chris Oswald — the tribunal as usurpation (a James 4 / Leviticus 19 sermon):

"Malicious judgments, whether we speak them or we keep them in our hearts, usurp the Judge. They unseat God as King. The critical spirit doesn't just attack the person sitting next to you, doesn't just attack the person in your care group — it attacks God." — Lord Willing

"The harshly judgmental person doesn't just attack his neighbor. He doesn't just attack the law. James says he attacks the Lawgiver." — Lord Willing

"It's not just a failure of relationship. It's a failure of reverence... You're also trying to be God. You're also acting as if you are God." — Lord Willing

And on the tribunal's gift for self-justification:

"Have you ever met a gossip or a slanderer or a grumbler who actually was quick to admit they were sinning?... They convince themselves, and they attempt to convince others, that the sins of their heart and their tongue drip righteousness — when really they spread fire." — Lord Willing

Austin Triplett — Simon's silent double verdict:

"He silently judges her. And in his judgment of her, he pronounces a judgment on Jesus." — Living Downstream (2024-09-01)

Jonathan Vogan — the tribunal in its most respectable robe, doctrinal precision:

"I discovered the doctrines of grace and was marked by anything but grace... Don't use discernment and defending the truth as an excuse to be [contentious]. Be aware of any tendency toward a critical spirit you may have." — Knowledge of God Leads to Worship of God (2020-12-27)

The page in one line

The silent tribunal is madon rehearsed in the diabolos' vacated courtroom — a coup against the one Lawgiver, conducted in a venue he audits, prosecuting cases he has already closed.

If You Can Keep Your Mind…

Profiles in Contention · No. 2 — opening Section I: The Man Against the Mob. An essay series from the Litigating Without a License project.

Rudyard Kipling's most famous poem begins with a line everyone knows and almost no one has taken seriously:

If you can keep your head when all about you Are losing theirs and blaming it on you…

Read the second line again, because that's where the poem stops being a greeting card. Kipling isn't just describing composure under pressure. He's describing what composure costs. When everyone around you loses their head, they do not admire the man who keeps his. They blame him for it. The calm one becomes the problem. His refusal to panic reads as arrogance; his refusal to join reads as betrayal. Kipling knew — decades before the family-systems people gave it clinical names — that a crowd losing its mind experiences the one sane man as an attack.

Keep your head, and the mob will come for it. Which is why I'd write the line for our purposes as: if you can keep your mind — because what a mob actually takes from you first is not your composure. It's your judgment.

Remember Edmund Ross from the introduction. In 1868 the whole apparatus of American public opinion — his party, his press, his constituents — had reached a verdict on Andrew Johnson, and the verdict was unanimous, and unanimity felt like proof. Ross's private mail ran to threats. And his answer, looking down into his "open grave," was the answer of a man whose mind was still his own: the case hasn't been proven. Not "the president is good." Not "you're all wrong about his character." Just: the evidence doesn't sustain the charge, and I will not lend my verdict to your momentum.

The Bible saw this coming about thirty-four centuries before Kansas. There is a commandment — an actual, black-letter commandment, sitting in the law of Moses two chapters after Sinai — that almost nobody has ever memorized:

"You shall not fall in with the many to do evil, nor shall you bear witness in a lawsuit, siding with the many, so as to pervert justice." (Exodus 23:2)

Notice two things. First, God thought this needed to be a law — the pull of the many is treated as a standing force, like gravity, that his people must be commanded against. Second, notice the setting: a lawsuit. The commandment assumes the mob's natural habitat is a courtroom — that when the many lose their minds together, what they produce is not chaos but a verdict. A mob is not the absence of judgment. It is judgment with the brakes cut: the silent tribunal of the individual heart gone corporate, with unanimity standing in for evidence.

And the Bible's most instructive mob story is not about the mob at all. It's about the man standing in front of it.

Aaron, or: out came this calf

Exodus 32. Moses has been up the mountain for forty days, and the people panic. "The people gathered themselves together against Aaron" — mark that preposition — "and said to him, 'Up, make us gods who shall go before us. As for this Moses… we do not know what has become of him.'"

Now, Aaron is not a villain waiting for his moment. He is the number-two man, a gifted communicator, soon to be Israel's first high priest — and he is standing alone in front of a frightened crowd of hundreds of thousands. And everything that happens next happens because Aaron does the math a certain way. He looks at the mob's anxiety and concludes that his job is to manage it. So he collects the gold. He fashions the calf. He even tries to sanctify the disaster — "he built an altar before it, and made a proclamation and said, 'Tomorrow shall be a feast to the LORD'" — pasting the covenant name of God over the idol like a permit slapped on a condemned building.

I preached a whole sermon on this scene once, and the phrase I couldn't escape was this one: Aaron's move is baptizing the people's unbelief. He didn't share the mob's panic. He blessed it. He took the crowd's anxious craving and did the theological paperwork to endorse it — and leaders have been repeating the maneuver ever since, finding biblical categories to sanctify whatever direction the herd is already running. The mob rarely asks its leaders for permission to sin plainly. It asks them for a feast day — a religious frame, a justifying vocabulary, an altar in front of the thing. And the leader who has quietly decided that his real job is keeping the crowd happy will find himself, sooner or later, holding an engraving tool and explaining the results.

His explanation, by the way, is the most darkly comic verse in the Old Testament. When Moses comes down and demands an account, Aaron says: "I said to them, 'Let any who have gold take it off.' So they gave it to me, and I threw it into the fire, and out came this calf" (32:24). Out came this calf. No craftsman, no choices, no engraving tool — just a fire that spontaneously produced idolatry. And here is the profile's uncomfortable center: Aaron may have half believed it. Men who fold before mobs almost never experience themselves as folding. They experience themselves as reasonable — as buying time, keeping the peace, staying at the table, managing a delicate situation. The self-deception is part of the failure. Nobody in the story thinks he's in the story.

Why does a man like Aaron fold? Not, usually, from villainy. From fear of the accusation that always comes for the one who won't — the crowd charging the resister with pride ("you think you're better than us" — the exact charge Korah will later fire at Moses). From false humility, which sounds spiritual and is really unbelief about one's assignment. From love of position — preferring to remain a leader in name over becoming a former leader in fact. Every one of those motives can quote a Bible verse. None of them can keep its mind.

Moses, or: the man against the mob

Now watch the counter-profile walk down the mountain.

Moses does not manage the situation. He names it — grinds the calf to powder, stands in the camp gate, and says the least mob-compatible sentence in the Pentateuch: "Who is on the LORD's side? Come to me" (32:26). Where Aaron treated the crowd as a force to be appeased, Moses treats it as a group of individual men, every one of whom is about to make a personal decision in full view of God. The mob de-mobs the instant someone refuses to address it as a mob.

But here is what keeps Moses from being merely the hero with the strong jaw: the man who defied the crowd to its face then climbs back up the mountain to defend it. "Alas, this people has sinned a great sin… But now, if you will forgive their sin — and if not, please blot me out of your book" (32:31–32). Confrontation downhill, intercession uphill. He would not flatter them before their faces, and he would not abandon them before God's. That is the full anatomy of the man against the mob, and it's the anatomy this whole series keeps finding: the one who keeps his mind is never merely a resister. He is an advocate. Ross voted to acquit. Moses offered to be blotted out. The pattern has a name, and a family resemblance — because there was another Man who stood silent before a screaming crowd ("Crucify him!" being the mob's masterpiece, and Pilate its Aaron, washing his hands while baptizing the verdict), and who spent his dying breath on the mob's own case file: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do."

They know not what they do. Even the Lord's verdict on the mob carries the Aaron insight: nobody in the mob thinks he's in the mob. Luke gives us the definitive comic footnote in Acts 19, where a riot chants "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!" for two solid hours — and the narrator remarks, deadpan, that "most of them did not know why they had come together" (19:32). That is a mob's honest biography, in one line, and it has been reprinted daily on the internet ever since.

Keeping your mind

So how does a person keep his mind when the room is losing its collective one? Not by temperament — Aaron presumably had a fine temperament. The men who stand share something else: they have settled, in advance, the question of whom they work for. The five words Aaron needed at the foot of the mountain — and the five words I have prescribed to every leader I've ever counseled — are these: I don't work for you. Said not in contempt but in clarity: I work for the Lord; I would love for you to join me in trusting him; but your anxiety is not my employer. Joshua, who watched the whole calf disaster as a young aide, said it at the end of his life with a house behind it: "Choose this day whom you will serve… as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD." And Jesus — who drew larger crowds than anyone and folded before none of them — carried the secret in one sentence of John's Gospel: "many believed in his name when they saw the signs… but Jesus did not entrust himself to them" (John 2:23–24). He loved crowds. He fed them, taught them, wept over them. He just never once let them hold his mind.

That is the whole program of Section I, and the profiles ahead — Korah with his flattering syllogism, the ten spies who captured a nation's nerve in one night, Absalom harvesting grievances at the gate — are all variations on the single question Exodus 23:2 puts to every generation: when the many have reached their verdict, and the verdict feels like proof, and the blame has already started sliding toward whoever hesitates — can you keep your mind?

The case hasn't been proven. Come to the gate.

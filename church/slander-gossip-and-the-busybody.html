Slander, Gossip, and the Busybody

Part of Litigating Without a License. Downstream of the Silent Tribunal: the busybody gathers evidence without jurisdiction, gossip circulates the case file, and slander publishes the verdict. Three stages of one unlicensed practice.

Working definition of slander

Slander is taking the information you see and understand, and developing a verdict about it that is defensible with the data but not the truth.

This definition matters because it removes slander's favorite alibi: "but it's true." Most slander does not fabricate evidence; it renders a false verdict on real evidence. The slanderer's facts frequently check out. His conclusion is the lie — and the pride underneath is a triple assumption: that he has all the data, that he commands all possible interpretations of the data, and that his explanation is the right one. Each assumption belongs to omniscience. That is why slander is theological before it is social: it borrows God's chair (see below).

The prototype is Numbers 13:32 — the spies "brought up an evil report (dibbah) of the land." Notice: their data were accurate. The cities were fortified; the people were large; Caleb saw the same grapes and giants. The dibbah was not in the observations but in the verdict — "we are not able" — which was defensible with the data and false about God. (And Numbers 14:36–37: the ten who spread the dibbah die by plague — Scripture's severity meter on the sin.) Thomas Watson saw that this is also the devil's own method: "He raises an evil report upon religion as those spies did on Canaan" (The Beatitudes — Appendix; see Diabolos — The Accuser).

Case study 1: Simon — slander in embryo (Luke 7:36–50)

Simon never speaks; the slander is complete before it is published (see The Internal Courtroom). Watch the mechanism against the definition:

  • The data: a woman "of the city," known reputation; physical contact — tears, hair, kisses, ointment; Jesus permitting all of it
  • The verdict: "If this man were a prophet, he would have known who and what sort of woman this is... for she is a sinner" (7:39)
  • The verdict is defensible. Given Simon's data, the inference is reasonable — a prophet should perceive defilement; this woman's record is genuinely bad. The syllogism is valid
  • And it is false at every joint — because Simon is missing two data points. Jesus does know who she is — he knows Simon's unspoken thoughts too, which is the scene's quiet proof of exactly the prophetic knowledge Simon denies. And the woman is not, in the decisive sense, "a sinner" — the missing datum, revealed at the end: "her sins, which are many, are forgiven" (7:47). The great love Simon read as evidence of her shame was actually evidence of her pardon
  • The irony completes the definition: Simon says Jesus lacks knowledge of "who and what sort of woman this is" — but it is Simon who does not know who she now is. The slanderer's charge of ignorance is a confession

Case study 2: Paul's slanderers — Romans 3:8

"And why not do evil that good may come? — as some people slanderously charge us (blasphēmoumetha) with saying. Their condemnation is just." (Rom 3:8; the objection returns in 6:1, "Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound?")

Run it through the definition:

  • The data: Paul preaches justification apart from works of the law (3:28); where sin increased, grace superabounded (5:20); the ungodly counted righteous by faith (4:5)
  • The verdict: "He must be teaching that we should sin so grace can abound — he is stirring up antinomianism"
  • Defensible with the data. The inference is so natural that Paul has to preempt it twice — a gospel of merit-free grace can be construed that way. (It is even a rough test of fidelity: a gospel that cannot be misheard as antinomian is probably not Paul's gospel)
  • And false. "By no means! How can we who died to sin still live in it?" (6:2). The conclusion was never Paul's; it was the slanderer's own construction, attributed to Paul and published as his position

Note where the false verdict comes from: the flesh attributes to the preacher the inference it would draw. From the corpus: "The person who hears the gospel in their flesh will very often think, oh, well, this is great news — I now get to do what I want... it's of such prominence and predictability that you can find almost every apostle, in almost every epistle, after declaring the pure gospel, emphasize the importance of practical holiness" (Chris Oswald, 1 John 3:1–18 Revisited, 2025-11-09). The slanderer of Romans 3:8 reads his own flesh into Paul's mouth. Slander is often autobiography with someone else's name on it.

And Paul's verdict on the verdict-makers — "their condemnation is just" — is the closed-court principle in reverse: the unlicensed judgment becomes the evidence in the licensed one (Matt 7:2; 12:36–37).

The most-slandered man

The definition explains the Gospels' strangest pattern: Jesus, with a perfect record, was slandered constantly — because slander needs only data, not guilt.

  • Matt 11:18–19 — John fasts: "He has a demon." Jesus feasts: "a glutton and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners." Opposite data, same verdict — proof that the tribunal's ruling precedes the evidence, which merely furnishes the brief. And the charge was data-defensible: he did eat with them. "Yet wisdom is justified by her deeds"
  • Mark 3:22 — the exorcisms were undeniable data; the verdict: "by the prince of demons he casts out the demons." Slander at its maximum: God's own work attributed to the devil — which is the context of the unforgivable-sin warning. The worst sin named in the Gospels is a slanderous interpretation of accurate data
  • 1 Sam 1:12–17 — the pattern's Old Testament miniature: Eli watches Hannah's lips move without sound at the sanctuary and rules, "How long will you be drunk?" Defensible; false; missing datum: "I have been pouring out my soul before the LORD." Even a priest at his post can run this play — and to his credit, he reverses on new evidence and blesses her

The counter-example: Joseph (Matt 1:18–20). Handed the most damning dataset imaginable — a betrothed found pregnant — Joseph, "being a just man," reaches the defensible verdict and refuses to publish it: "unwilling to put her to shame, resolved to divorce her quietly." Justice, in Matthew's vocabulary, includes protecting the accused from exposure even while you believe the data. And then God supplies the missing datum by revelation — vindicating not only Mary but Joseph's restraint. The righteous man holds his verdicts loosely and his mercy firmly, because he knows the file is incomplete.

The epistemology of pride

The triple assumption, with its answering texts:

  1. "I have all the data." — "If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame" (Prov 18:13). "The one who states his case first seems right, until the other comes and examines him" (Prov 18:17 — Scripture institutionalizes cross-examination because one-party data always convinces). "Do not pronounce judgment before the time... the Lord will disclose the purposes of the heart" (1 Cor 4:5 — decisive data is structurally unavailable until the Judge arrives). "Man looks on the outward appearance" (1 Sam 16:7)
  2. "I command all interpretations." — Job's friends own a complete interpretive grid (suffering ⇒ sin), and it is airtight, venerable, and wrong — "you have not spoken of me what is right" (Job 42:7). "Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment" (John 7:24)
  3. "My explanation is the truth." — "The way of a fool is right in his own eyes" (Prov 12:15); "Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Prov 26:12)

Love runs the opposite epistemology: it "does not reckon (logizetai) the evil" — an accounting term; love keeps no ledger of construals — and it "believes all things, hopes all things" (1 Cor 13:5, 7). Where slander selects the worst defensible interpretation and publishes it as fact, love selects the best defensible interpretation and holds even that provisionally. Both read the same data. The difference is which advocate the reader works for (you are either an accuser or an advocate — the fork applied to interpretation).

Gossip: the case file in circulation

Gossip is the distribution network between the silent verdict and the public one. Its anatomy in Proverbs:

  • Prov 26:20–22 — "where there is no whisperer, contention (madon) ceases"; the whisperer's words are "delicious morsels" that "go down into the inner parts." Gossip is madon's fuel line — cut it and the fire dies
  • Prov 16:28 — "a whisperer separates close friends"; 11:13; 20:19 — the rakil reveals secrets; Lev 19:16 — banned in the same breath as heart-hatred and frank reasoning (vv. 16–17): the whisper is the evasion of the licensed process
  • The hearer is a co-conspirator: "In slander as well as robbery, the receiver is as bad as the thief. If there were not gratified hearers of slanders, there would be an end of the trade... 'The tale-bearer carries the devil in his tongue, and the tale-hearer carries the devil in his ear!'" (Spurgeon, quoting Trapp, Treasury of David — Psalm 15)
  • And it never feels like slander from inside — from the corpus, a first-person anatomy: "In my desire to cozy up to my buddy with sweet morsels and rumors, I had led us both into gossip and slander... I felt close to my friend as we whispered to each other. But in reality it was driving a wedge between both of us and the brother we were maligning with sanctified language... in my blindness, I'd convinced myself that the fire was actually nice and warm — 'Roast some marshmallows!'" (Chris Oswald, Lord Willing)

The busybody: discovery without jurisdiction

The busybody is the tribunal's evidence-gathering arm — the discovery phase of cases she has no license to try. That is why 1 Tim 5:13 pairs phlyaroi and periergoi in one clause: the gossip needs inventory, and the busybody supplies it, "going about from house to house."

  • 1 Pet 4:15 — allotriepiskopos, the self-ordained overseer of another's household, filed between "thief" and "evildoer." (MacArthur: "Good grief, how did a busybody get in there with a murderer and a thief?" — Keys to Spiritual Growth, Part 2 — Peter's answer: same genus, jurisdiction theft)
  • 2 Thess 3:11 — "not busy at work, but busybodies" — idleness is the busybody's enabling condition; the empty docket goes looking for cases
  • Prov 26:17 — "whoever meddles in a quarrel not his own is like one who takes a passing dog by the ears"
  • John 21:21–22 — the Lord's own ruling on the jurisdiction question: "Lord, what about this man?" — "What is that to you? You follow me."
  • The antidote is the hēsychia cluster: "aspire to live quietly, and to mind your own affairs, and to work with your hands" (1 Thess 4:11); "do their work quietly" (2 Thess 3:12). From the corpus: "There's just certain rules for unity, and one of them is: just stay busy, do your work, mind your own business" (Chris Oswald, Marriage & The Mission of God, 2026-03-15, on Titus 2). The same word-family describes the wife's "gentle and quiet spirit" (1 Pet 3:4) — one virtue answering busybody, gossip, and eshet midyanim at once

The pipeline, assembled

  1. The busybody collects data outside her jurisdiction (allotriepiskopos — discovery)
  2. The silent tribunal renders the defensible-but-false verdict (see The Internal Courtroom)
  3. Gossip circulates the case file in whispers (nirgan — the fuel of madon, Prov 26:20)
  4. Slander publishes the verdict as fact (katalalia — and the mouth that does it takes the name diabolos, 1 Tim 3:11)
  5. Every stage is the accuser's closed caseload, worked by volunteers — prosecuting in absentia, on partial data, cases the true court has already ruled on (Rom 8:33–34)

The page in one line

Slander is a verdict that survives the data but not the truth; gossip is that verdict in circulation; the busybody is its research department — and all three assume an omniscience that belongs to the only Judge who, knowing every datum, chose to justify.

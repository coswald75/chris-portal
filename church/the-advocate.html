The Advocate (Paraklētos) — Called Alongside to Call Out

Part of Litigating Without a License. The facing page to Diabolos — The Accuser. The courtroom has two speaking roles left, and this is the other one — held first by the Son, then by the Spirit, then deputized to every saint who opens his mouth about another saint.

The word

Paraklētos: from para (alongside) + kaleō (to call) — one called to your side; in Greek legal usage, the friend-advocate who stands with the accused, the counsel for the defense (Latin advocatus — "called to"). But play the compound literally, both directions, and you get the whole ministry: one who comes alongside and calls out. Called to your side; calling at your side. The Advocate's two motions are proximity and proclamation — he draws near, and he says things over you.

And the word-family is not exotic. Parakaleō / paraklēsis is the New Testament's ordinary vocabulary for encourage / encouragement (and comfort, and exhort). The title of the third person of the Trinity and the plainest word in church life are the same word. Every act of encouragement is small-p paraclete work — junior counsel, speaking for the defense.

Two Advocates, two courtrooms

  • 1 John 2:1 — "if anyone sins, we have an Advocate (paraklēton) with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous" — counsel of record in heaven's court
  • John 14:16 — "another Paraklētos" — another implies the first; the Spirit continues in us the office the Son holds above us
  • John 15:26; Rom 8:16 — his method is testimony: "he will bear witness about me"; "the Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God" — defense testimony delivered inside the internal courtroom, the one venue where the disbarred accuser still solicits standing
  • Rom 8:26–27 + 8:34 — both benches covered: the Spirit "intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" below, Christ "intercedes for us" above. The believer's case is never unrepresented in either court, at any hour (Heb 7:25) — the day-and-night accusation (Rev 12:10) answered by round-the-clock counsel
  • John 16:8–11 — and note the one prosecution the Spirit runs: he convicts the world — and the third count is "the ruler of this world is judged." The Advocate's only prosecutorial act ends with the accuser in the dock

The Advocate's threefold sight

Here is what makes his advocacy something other than wishful thinking. The Spirit sees the person in three tenses at once, and testifies from the whole file:

As he was — "dead in trespasses and sins" (Eph 2:1); "foolish, disobedient, led astray, slaves to various passions... hated by others and hating one another" (Titus 3:3). The Advocate does not airbrush the record — he is the one who convicted you of it (John 16:8). Advocacy that denies the rap sheet is sentimentality, not defense. His defense is built on a paid record, not a cleaned one.

As he is — "and such were some of you. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God" (1 Cor 6:11); blood-bought (Acts 20:28; 1 Pet 1:18–19); "being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another. For this comes from the Lord who is the Spirit" (2 Cor 3:18). Notice the double role: the witness is also the craftsman. The Spirit testifies to a sanctification he is personally executing. When he says of a stumbling saint "this one is being conformed to the image of the Son," it is not a hopeful forecast — it is a contractor describing his own job site.

As he will be — "those whom he justified he also glorified" (Rom 8:29–30 — past tense; in the decree it is already done); "he who began a good work in you will bring it to completion" (Phil 1:6); "we shall be like him" (1 John 3:2). The Advocate has read the final page of the file. His confidence about the defendant's end is not optimism; it is access to the verdict.

This is why Paul can write "He will sustain you to the end, guiltless" to Corinth, of all churches (1 Cor 1:8) — "this church was anything but strong at present. How could he write with integrity, 'He will keep you strong to the end'?... his confidence in the faithfulness of God" (C.J. Mahaney, Grace and the Adventure of Leadership). Paul was reading the file the way the Spirit reads it.

Lewis: the weight of the future glory

"It is a serious thing to live in a society of possible gods and goddesses, to remember that the dullest and most uninteresting person you can talk to may one day be a creature which, if you saw it now, you would be strongly tempted to worship... There are no ordinary people. You have never talked to a mere mortal." — C.S. Lewis, "The Weight of Glory" (1941)

The advocate's eye is eschatological. The accuser reads the defendant backward (the record); the Advocate reads him forward (the decree). Every Christian you find irritating, disappointing, or slow is a pre-release version of a creature you would be tempted to kneel to. To speak of him now as though the present draft were the final text is not just unkind — it is bad reporting on the file.

Barnabas: the human case study

The early church nicknamed a man after this office: Joseph of Cyprus, "who was called by the apostles Barnabas — huios paraklēseōs, son of encouragement" (Acts 4:36). Watch him do the work:

  • Acts 9:26–27 — Jerusalem faces a data-defensible verdict: Saul the persecutor claims conversion, "and they were all afraid of him, not believing." The data supported the fear — the same epistemology, defensive edition. "But Barnabas took him and brought him to the apostles and declared how he had seen the Lord." Advocacy against reasonable suspicion — testimony to grace the record didn't yet show. No Barnabas, humanly speaking, no Paul
  • Acts 11:23–24 — the paraclete method in a single verse: "he came and saw the grace of God, and was glad, and he exhorted (parekalei) them all" — saw grace, then called it out. The advocate's eye precedes the advocate's mouth. And Luke's explanation: "for he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit" — of course he was; the encourager is possessed by the Encourager
  • Acts 15:36–39 → 2 Tim 4:11; Col 4:10 — Barnabas advocates for John Mark against damning data (desertion, Acts 13:13), at the cost of his partnership with Paul. Years later, Paul himself files the reversal: "Get Mark... he is very useful to me for ministry." Barnabas's advocacy was vindicated by the man Mark became — and the church got a Gospel out of the defendant

"Barnabas is so encouraging. He just breathes life into people." — Alec Shoffeitt, Shining in the Shadows (2023-06-18)

Encouragement and affirmation: deputized advocacy

If paraklēsis is the Advocate's own word, then the church's encouragement ministry is the Spirit's courtroom practice distributed to the whole body:

  • Heb 3:13 — "exhort (parakaleite) one another every day... that none of you may be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin." Daily paraclesis is the countermeasure to daily accusation — the accuser files day and night (Rev 12:10), so the defense must not take days off. An unencouraged saint is a defendant whose counsel stopped showing up
  • Heb 10:24–25; 1 Thess 5:11, 14 — "consider how to stir up one another"; "encourage one another and build one another up"
  • Eph 4:29 — the speech standard: "only such as is good for building up, as fits the occasion, that it may give grace to those who hear" — words as grace-delivery systems
  • Prov 12:25 — "Anxiety weighs down the heart, but a good word makes it glad" — the good word answers the quiet indictment of Accusing God at the level of the neighbor's heart
  • 1 Cor 1:4–9 — Paul's pattern: thanksgiving for observed grace before correction, even at Corinth

Affirmation is evidence-reading, not flattery. To affirm a believer is to name, specifically and truthfully, evidence of the Spirit's workmanship in him — to testify to work in progress on the craftsman's own job site. That is why it is neither optional nor soft:

"When you are discerning evidences of grace, and when you are communicating that discernment, you are not simply emulating Paul's example — you are imitating God himself." — C.J. Mahaney, Grace and the Adventure of Leadership

"Which are you more aware of — evidences of grace, or areas in need of growth?... Husbands, you need to at some point ask your wife: what do I make you more aware of?" — Mahaney, same message (and on children: "If your child is more aware of areas needing adjustment than evidences of grace, you will eventually exasperate your child")

"Only the humble can identify evidences of grace in those in need of adjustment. The proud are incapable of doing that." — Mahaney, same message

Recommended reading: Sam Crabtree, Practicing Affirmation (Crossway, 2011) — the book-length treatment of exactly this: God-centered praise of those who are not God. Crabtree's thesis is that specific, truthful commendation of Christ's work in people refreshes them and honors God — and that leaders and parents should watch their ratio of affirmation to correction the way an advocate watches his case file. Pair it with this page and the Mahaney message above.

And the live link to prayer: encouragement is advocacy toward the saint; intercession is advocacy about the saint — prayer as the licensed form of having a case about someone. The full paraclete pattern is both: come alongside the brother and call grace out over him; then go to the bench and plead his cause. Speak to him for God, and to God for him.

The page in one line

The Paraklētos is the one called alongside who calls out — reading every saint in three tenses from a file that ends in glory — and every believer who encourages, affirms, or intercedes is practicing law in his firm: there are no ordinary defendants.

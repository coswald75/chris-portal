The powers' hold on people ran through accusation, and their case was true. This is the point at which the cosmic and the pastoral halves of this wiki turn out to be one thing: the cheirographon of Col 2:14 — the handwritten IOU with its legal demands — is precisely what a tormented conscience believes is still outstanding.

Zechariah 3

Joshua the high priest stands in filthy garments with Satan at his right hand to accuse him. Nothing in the scene suggests the garments are clean or the accusation false. The verdict does not turn on the evidence. The Lord rebukes the accuser, and the garments are exchanged.

This is the model for how the answer has to be given. Not you are not as bad as he says, which is untrue and which the conscience knows is untrue. Rather: the charge has been dealt with by someone else, and the prosecutor has no standing to bring it.

Two kinds of blood

Hebrews 12:24 sets Christ's blood against Abel's, and says it speaks a better word. Abel's blood accuses. It cries from the ground for a reckoning and it is entirely in the right to do so. Christ's blood speaks too — it is not silent, and it is not merely a covering — but what it says is acquittal.

Both are testimony. The question is which testimony the court is hearing, and that was settled at Calvary.

Disbarred, not silenced

Col 2:15 does not say the accuser stopped talking. He was disarmed and shamed. He still prosecutes; he simply has no case and no standing, and the ruling has already been entered. This matters pastorally, because a person told that the accusations would stop, and then hearing them, will conclude the gospel failed. Told instead that the prosecutor was disbarred and is shouting outside the courthouse, they have somewhere to put the experience.

Luther's counsel to Jerome Weller runs the same way: when the devil raises the subject of your sins, do not take up the defence of your record — concede it and point at Christ.

Paraphrased; see Luther's letters, 1530

The accuser inside

Colossians 2:16–18 — let no one pass judgement on you, let no one disqualify you — is aimed at teachers imposing rules from outside. The scrupulous person imposes them from inside. The logic covers the case, but this is application rather than exegesis, and it should be said out loud rather than slid past.

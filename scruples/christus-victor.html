Christus Victor names the theme of Christ's triumph over the powers. The interesting question is almost never whether he won. It is how — and the answer this wiki takes is that the victory was juridical before it was anything else.

One sentence, not two

Colossians 2:14 and 15 are usually preached apart: forgiveness in one, cosmic victory in the other. They are joined. The cheirographon, the handwritten record of debt with its legal demands, is set aside and nailed to the cross — and by that same act the rulers and authorities are disarmed and paraded.

The powers' weapon was never force. It was standing. They had a case, and the case was true. Cancel the record and you have not overpowered the prosecutor; you have disbarred him. He may still shout, and he does, but he is shouting about a matter already ruled on. See Accusation.

Deep magic

Lewis's version of this in The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe is the most useful illustration available, partly because it is already in most people's heads. The Witch's claim on the traitor is legitimate and Aslan does not dispute it. He satisfies it. Chapter 13 establishes that the law is real; chapter 15 reveals an older law underneath it by which a willing and innocent substitute causes death itself to run backwards.

What makes it exactly right for this argument is that the Witch loses on the law's own terms. She is not defeated by a bigger lion. She is defeated by a deeper statute she did not know about, which is the shape of Col 2:14–15 and the reason the ransom theory is a wrong turn: nothing is paid to the enemy.

Where this sits with other models. Christus Victor is treated here as the public face of penal substitution rather than an alternative to it. The debt is really paid; the parade follows because the debt is really paid. Preaching the triumph without the courtroom produces a verdict with no ground — the failure mode diagnosed on Scrupulosity.

The promise it fulfils

Genesis 3:15 is the eviction notice. The serpent's whole strategy, on the hermit crab reading, was occupancy: unable to make anything, he moved into what God made — law, wisdom, justice, religion, marriage, government — and wore them. The promised seed does not destroy those structures. He takes them back, one at a time, by entering them lawfully: born under the law, fulfilling all righteousness, embodying the wisdom that philosophy was squatting in, satisfying the justice the accuser was administering.

A Word from Pastor Chris

Thanks for interacting with this material. This is a collection of concepts I've pulled together from various experiences and reading sources over the years. It's written out of a desire to give theological leaders the capacity to walk with people through what is often known as scrupulosity — or what used to be called scrupulosity. I have noticed over the years that a lot of people who claim to be discerning lack basic self-awareness. All of this content is meant to equip you, the spiritual leader, in your effort to help the so-called highly discerning discern themselves more accurately.

Section One

The Word

Latin scrupulus — a small, sharp pebble; the stone in your sandal. Also the smallest apothecary weight (1/24 of an ounce). Both images say the same thing: a scruple is a tiny thing that torments out of all proportion to its actual weight. The sufferer cannot walk freely because of a stone no one else can see.

Section Two

Classic Definitions

Jeremy Taylor, Ductor Dubitantium (1660):

"A scruple is a great trouble of mind proceeding from a little motive, and a great indisposition, by which the conscience, though sufficiently determined by proper arguments, dares not proceed to action."

Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), distilling centuries of moral theology:

"An unfounded apprehension and consequently unwarranted fear that something is a sin which, as a matter of fact, is not."

Note what both definitions insist on: the conscience is already sufficiently answered. Scrupulosity is not a lack of information; it is an inability — or a refusal — to rest in the answer.

Section Three

The Taxonomy of Conscience

The older writers (Catholic moral theology; Perkins and the Puritan casuists) treated conscience as something that can be well or sick, and mapped its diseases:

StateDescription
Good / tenderRightly informed, responsive to real sin, able to rest when forgiven
DoubtingGenuinely unsure whether an act is lawful; needs instruction
ScrupulousSufficiently answered but cannot rest; sees sin where there is none
ErringConfidently wrong — calls evil good or good evil
Seared / laxUnder-responsive; excuses real sin (the opposite disease)
Why Are Catholics Included in This Content?

As diagnosticians of the conscience and its diseases, the Catholic casuists are centuries deep and largely right. As architects of assurance, they build on the wrong foundation. We take the map, not the medicine cabinet.

Key insight: scrupulosity and tenderness are not the same thing, and scrupulosity is not extra holiness. It is tenderness diseased — conscience misfiring on trifles, which over time can actually dull it toward weightier matters.

This is Paul's territory, and he names it twice:

  • Colossians 2:20–23 — "touch not, taste not, handle not." Scrupulosity manufactures regulations "according to human precepts and teachings." And Paul diagnoses the misapprehension underneath: such rules "have indeed an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-made religion and asceticism and severity to the body, but they are of no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh" (v. 23). The scrupulous instinct believes that severity to the body restrains sin. Paul says it doesn't — it only looks wise. Rule-multiplication is a failed technology for holiness.
  • Romans 14 / 1 Corinthians 8 — the weak conscience. Paul's "weaker brother" is essentially the scrupulous conscience in community: bound where God has left free (food, days, drink). Two implications at once: the strong must not despise or wound the weak conscience — and weakness is named as weakness, something to be borne patiently but not enshrined as superior devotion. The weak brother is received, not imitated.

Section Four

The Mixed Motivations of Scrupulosity — Five Headwaters

The governing thesis: no scrupulous person is ever fully rooted in only one of these. It is never purely a wounded love of holiness, and never purely a play for self-righteousness. Every real case is a mixture, in proportions that shift over time — which is why diagnosis requires patience and why the sufferer himself is usually the worst judge of his own proportions. We present these as five headwaters feeding one river.

Headwater 1 — The malformation of a good thing.Patron: Richard Sibbes

Sibbes (The Bruised Reed, 1630, "Duties and Discouragements") handles scrupulosity as a disease that only afflicts something living:

  • Scruples vex the godly "even against the best duties; partly by disease of body, helped by Satan's malice." The attack lands precisely on prayer, the Supper, assurance — the best things.
  • Scrupulosity is "a sign of a godly soul, as some weeds are of a good soil." Only a conscience that cares about God can be sick this way. This affliction, rightly read, can be evidence for grace, not against it.
  • It springs more from "disordered imagination" than from conscience proper. The conscience is being tyrannized by the imagination, not informed by it. (Remarkably close to the modern account: intrusive thought ≠ moral verdict.)
  • Sins of infirmity vs. reigning sin: infirmities occur "whilst our judgment is overcast with the cloud of a sudden temptation," followed by grief and striving. "Weaknesses… may be consistent with boldness with God."
  • Frame over everything: Christ "will not quench the smoking flax" (Isa. 42:3 / Matt. 12:20).

This is the most credit we can give the scrupulous — and it is real credit. But it is not the whole story.

Headwater 2 — The desire to earn righteousness before God.Patron: Martin Luther

Luther is another head. His Anfechtungen — the hours-long confessions over trifles, the terror after absolution — were not merely a tender conscience mistreated. By his own later diagnosis, they were works-righteousness operating inside the conscience: the refusal to be justified by another, the insistence on assembling a righteousness of one's own, experienced as anxiety rather than pride. Staupitz saw it: "God is not angry with you — you are angry with God."

Here scrupulosity is not a malformed love of God but a disguised distrust of God — endless self-examination as a bid to bring something to the table. The scruple says "I must be sure I have done enough," and "enough" is a word grace does not use. The sufferer looks humble; the engine is self-salvation. Luther's liberation was not gentler self-scrutiny but the death of the whole project: an alien righteousness, received.

Headwater 3 — The desire to appear righteous before others.Patron: the young John Bunyan

A third stream: scrupulosity as differentiation — self-righteousness aimed horizontally rather than vertically. The multiplied rules, the visible abstentions, the anguished conscientiousness function (however subtly) as a way of standing out from, and above, other believers. My scruples prove my seriousness; your liberty proves your laxity.

A telltale symptom: the judgmental attitude. A tendency to notice others who lack your scruple — and to notice them in a way that tempts you toward self-righteousness. The scruple installs a private measuring stick, and the eye starts running it over everyone in the room: who else fasts, who lets their kids watch that, who came to the Table so casually. The noticing itself can feel like discernment, even like concern — but its fruit is comparison, and comparison's fruit is contempt (Luke 18:9 again: "trusted in themselves… and treated others with contempt"). A diagnostic question: does your scruple make you more aware of your own sin, or of other people's?

Patron: the young John Bunyan. Before Grace Abounding becomes the classic account of tormented scruple, it opens with a period Bunyan later diagnosed with brutal honesty: an outward reformation performed for an audience. His neighbors "did marvel much to see such a great and famous alteration in my life and manners… they began to praise, to commend, and to speak well of me, both to my face, and behind my back." His verdict on himself: "I loved to be talked of as one that was truly godly. I was proud of my godliness; and indeed, I did all I did, either to be seen of, or to be well spoken of, by men" (Grace Abounding, §§31–32). And note what his religion consisted of in that season: scruples over adiaphora — he gave up bell-ringing because "my conscience beginning to be tender, I thought such practice was but vain" (§33; it took him a year more to surrender dancing). Multiplied scruple and performed godliness in the same man at the same time — self-diagnosed. What broke the spell was overhearing the poor women of Bedford talking of the new birth "as if joy did make them speak": beside real, inward religion, he saw his own was display. Why he works as patron: he is regenerate (symmetry with Sibbes, Luther, Cowper preserved), self-aware (like Luther, he names his own engine), and his later agonies (see the case studies below) prove the mixture thesis in one biography — the man performing scruples at twenty is the man drowning in them at thirty. Behind him stand the biblical archetypes: the Pharisee of Luke 18:9–14 — and Saul of Tarsus, whose own testimony is explicitly comparative: "advancing in Judaism beyond many of my own age" (Gal. 1:14); "if anyone else thinks he has reason for confidence in the flesh, I have more" (Phil. 3:4–6) — credentials assembled for ranking, later counted as rubbish.

A canonical illustration (not the patron): Peter at Antioch (Gal. 2:11–14). Peter ate freely with Gentile believers — until "certain men came from James," and he "drew back and separated himself, fearing the circumcision party": scruple performed for an audience, conduct calibrated to the strict men in the room. Note the contagion — "even Barnabas was led astray"; performed scruple recruits, it bends the whole room. And Paul's rebuke lands exactly where our theology does: "not in step with the truth of the gospel," answered not with a rule but with justification itself (Gal. 2:16). We keep Peter here as an illustration rather than the patron — his story is bigger than this stream (see the excursus below).

Headwater 4 — An unwell body and mind.Patron: William Cowper

Sibbes already named "disease of body" as a contributing cause, and Taylor lists melancholy, sleeplessness, and solitariness among the springs of scruples. The tradition never treated this as purely a spiritual problem.

William Cowper (pronounced "Cooper," 1731–1800) is our patron here: hymn-writer with John Newton at Olney, author of "God Moves in a Mysterious Way," and a man repeatedly broken by depression and psychosis — including the fixed delusion that he alone was irrevocably damned. The man who wrote

"Judge not the Lord by feeble sense, but trust him for his grace…
God is his own interpreter, and he will make it plain"

could not, in his darkest seasons, apply a word of it to himself. Cowper teaches us two things at once: that a genuinely gracious soul can be tormented by an unwell mind without forfeiting grace, and that no amount of correct theology self-administered will medicate a broken brain.

Under this headwater belongs the modern clinical picture — and here we can name names, because there are well-known categories of mental illness that manifest in religious directions:

  • Obsessive-compulsive disorder — scrupulosity proper in the clinical sense ("the doubting disease"): obsessive fear of sin with compulsive confession, reassurance-seeking, and mental rituals. The closest match to the older writers' descriptions.
  • Depressive disorders — guilt, worthlessness, and damnation themes; the conviction of being uniquely beyond mercy (Cowper's fixed belief that he alone was irrevocably damned).
  • Bipolar mania — religious grandiosity: special missions, special revelations, sleepless devotional excess.
  • Schizophrenia-spectrum and psychotic disorders — religious voices, visions, and delusions; also postpartum psychosis, which not infrequently takes religious shape.

Headwater 5 — Spiritual warfare: the accuser of the brethren.Patron: Martin Luther

The fifth stream is named by Scripture itself: "the accuser of our brothers… who accuses them day and night before our God" (Rev. 12:10). Sibbes already pointed here — scruples vex the godly "partly by disease of body, helped by Satan's malice." The tradition never treated scrupulosity as a purely internal affair: there is an external intelligence with a stake in the believer's misery, and some accusation is neither your conscience, nor your motives, nor your brain chemistry. It is incoming fire.

The scene over this stream: Zechariah 3:1–5. Joshua the high priest stands in filthy garments, "and Satan standing at his right hand to accuse him." Everything in the scene teaches. The accusation is technically accurate — the garments really are filthy; Satan's best accusations are true facts wielded toward a false verdict. And the LORD does not dispute the facts. He rebukes the accuser ("The LORD rebuke you… is not this a brand plucked from the fire?") and changes the clothes. Justification is the answer to accusation — not a better argument, a better robe.

The relief-valve function. This stream matters pastorally precisely because of the question headwater 4 raises: once we say "some of this is illness," and headwaters 2–3 say "some of this is sin," the sufferer may conclude that every accusing thought is either his pathology or his fault. Stream 5 relieves that: a believer under relentless accusation may be neither self-righteous nor sick — he may be under attack. Peter (again) puts anxiety and adversary in adjacent verses: "casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you. Be sober-minded; be watchful. Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion… Resist him, firm in your faith" (1 Pet. 5:7–9).

Discernment. Satan's accusation is the counterfeit of the Spirit's conviction, and the conviction-vs-accusation distinction (below) is the test: conviction is specific, scriptural, drives toward Christ, and lifts on repentance. Accusation is relentless, aims at despair or at distance from Christ, keeps prosecuting after the verdict is in, and is never satisfied by the gospel's answer. The Spirit convicts sins that were repented of once; the accuser re-tries closed cases.

Patron: Martin Luther. Luther did not experience his Anfechtungen as abstract psychology; he experienced them as the devil at close quarters — accusations pressed in the night with a prosecutor's persistence, which he answered out loud. His counsel to his melancholy student Jerome Weller (1530) is this stream's classic tactic: "So when the devil throws your sins in your face and declares that you deserve death and hell, tell him this: I admit that I deserve death and hell, what of it? For I know One who suffered and made satisfaction on my behalf. His name is Jesus Christ, Son of God, and where he is there I shall be also." The move is not to dispute the charge but to change advocates: "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies. Who is to condemn?" (Rom. 8:33–34); "we have an advocate with the Father" (1 John 2:1).

Walk-on: Bunyan's Valley of the Shadow (The Pilgrim's Progress) — the fiend that "stepped up softly to him, and whisperingly suggested many grievous blasphemies to him, which he verily thought had proceeded from his own mind." The old literature already knew what the sufferer most needs told: some intrusive thoughts are neither yours nor from you — and you are not guilty of the whispers of another.

Section Five

Always a Mixture

You may have noticed Luther appeared twice. That is the point. These are streams, not stalls — no man is assigned to one; the waters intermix in him. Luther's scrupulosity was sourced in works-righteousness (2) and in satanic assault (5), by his own testimony — with no small contribution from constitution (4) besides.

Bunyan is a similar case. He enters this material as patron of stream 3 — the young man performing his scruples for the neighbors. But the man of Grace Abounding ten years on is unrecognizable from that file: a tender conscience (1) in an unwell mind (4), under an assailant he explicitly called the tempter (5). Same man, same besetting subject matter, radically shifted proportions. Diagnose Bunyan at twenty and file him under "performance," and you would have been right — and useless to him at thirty. Cowper's illness (4) delivered its verdicts in the accuser's voice (5) — a delusion whose entire content was damnation. And Peter — see the excursus below — is what all the waters look like moving through one turbulent riverbed. The streams are distinguishable at their springs; in an actual soul they run as one river. You treat the mixture as it presently runs, not a category label.

The same discipline applies to the modern take (stream 4's territory). "It's straight-up OCD" is probably too clean a diagnosis — exactly as too clean as "it's just a tender soul," "it's just works-righteousness," "it's just performance," or "it's just the devil." And we should be precise about what the psychological disciplines are actually doing: not naming essences, but attempting to name phenomena — clusters of observable experience — and, in particular, the biological connections to those phenomena. That work is real and useful as far as it goes. But a named phenomenon plus its biological correlates is not a complete account of a person, and medicine can run the monocausal reduction as confidently as moralism can. The pastoral cost is identical either way: whatever parts of the mixture the label doesn't cover go untreated. The old writers hold a quiet advantage here — not better science, but better instincts about mixture: Sibbes names body, imagination, and Satan's malice in a single sentence, and treats all three.

Note to Counselor

Your own personality and experiences can bias you toward seeing this complex situation through an oversimplified lens. If your strength is helping people in spiritual warfare, you will tend to find stream 5. If your education runs toward the biological causations, you will be tempted in that direction. If your formative experiences involved scrupulosity brought on by parenting, or by a particular church culture, you will reach for that template. Be careful. And hold both halves of the same truth: no matter how troubled an individual is — however real the biology — he still suffers from the same basic issues of pride, self-righteousness, and unbelief as everyone else. Likewise, a person with obvious issues of pride or self-differentiation may also have real temperamental or physical issues in play. The mixture runs in both directions — and so must the counselor's suspicion of his own first instinct.

Excursus

Peter: A Turbulent Man with a Turbulent Faith

Placed after the five streams — temperament, like the body (headwater 4), is constitutional — and kept outside the streams proper, so the streams stay intact. Peter is not the patron of any one headwater; he is what a real conscience looks like with all the waters moving through it — and he opens the question of temperament.

The thesis: temperament is the riverbed the headwaters flow through. It doesn't create the water, but it shapes the course, the speed, and the noise. The classic writers knew this in their own idiom — Taylor and the Catholic directors name melancholy as the temperament that breeds scruples: slow, inward, deliberating, self-examining. Peter is the opposite constitution — sanguine, forward, turbulent — and his story shows that turbulence produces its own disorders of conscience: not the melancholic's endless inward circling, but oscillation (overconfidence and collapse, refusal and overcorrection) and a conscience acutely calibrated to the audience in the room. Temperament belongs near headwater 4 — it is constitutional, not chosen, and not itself sin — but it is the milder cousin of illness: a given, to be known and governed, not repented of as such.

The stations of a turbulent conscience:

  • The boat (Luke 5:8) — "Depart from me, for I am a sinful man." The proto-scrupulous move: sin-awareness creating distance from Christ instead of flight to him. Jesus: "Do not be afraid." Note: here Peter seems primarily informed by Sibbes's version of scrupulosity — his conscience is triggered and not soothed, in part because he has yet to understand the gracious nature of the God who has drawn near to him.
  • The boast (Matt. 26:33) — "Though they all fall away, I never will." Comparative confidence; self-differentiation above the brothers. The boast and the courtyard are one story — the collapse reveals what the confidence was made of.
  • The sifting (Luke 22:31–32) — "Simon, Simon, behold, Satan demanded to have you, that he might sift you like wheat, but I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail. And when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers." Stream 5 in Peter's own biography: the accuser asks for the turbulent man by name. Note what Christ does and does not do — he does not cancel the sifting; he intercedes through it. The believer's security under accusation is not his own stability but Christ's prayer. And the restoration is scheduled before the failure ("when you have turned again") — with a ministry already assigned on the far side of it: strengthen your brothers.
  • The courtyard — fear of man at its rawest. Not scruple itself, but the root system that later fruits as scruple at Antioch. Fear of man survives even Pentecost.
  • The basin (John 13:8–10) — "You shall never wash my feet" → "not my feet only, but my hands and my head!" Textbook scrupulous oscillation: refusing grace out of misplaced reverence, then overcorrecting into excess, unable to find where grace actually sits. And Christ's answer is the whole assurance structure: "The one who has bathed does not need to wash, except for his feet — he is completely clean." Definitive cleansing vs. daily cleansing — precisely the distinction the scrupulous conscience cannot hold.
  • The rooftop (Acts 10) — lodging with a tanner (God loosening the scruple before the sheet ever descends), then the most scrupulous sentence in Scripture: "Not so, Lord" — refusing God on the grounds of devotion to God, three times. And the correcting word that may be THE scrupulosity text of the Bible: "What God has made clean, do not call common." The disease in one clause — and it reaches past food laws to the conscience itself (Heb. 10:22; 1 John 3:20).
  • The council and the relapse (Acts 15 → Gal. 2) — "why put a yoke on the disciples' necks?… we will be saved through grace, just as they will" — and then, at Antioch, relapse into the very scruple God had personally revoked for him. Time-series proof of the mixture thesis: the proportions shift; deliverance is not immunity.
  • The beach (John 21:21–22) — freshly restored, the comparing eye: "Lord, what about this man?" And the standing remedy for comparison: "What is that to you? You follow me."
  • The letters — the healed man's voice: "Cast all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" (1 Pet. 5:7). The turbulent man's whole biography compressed into one pastoral sentence.
  • The mountain (Matt. 17:1–8; Mark 9:5–6) — final station, out of chronological order on purpose. "Let us make three tents… for he did not know what to say, for they were terrified." Pure turbulence: overwhelmed by glory, Peter reaches for a religious project — anxious doing as a response to holy fear. And the Father's correction is the exact solution to the whole biography, and to scrupulosity itself: "This is my beloved Son… listen to him." The turbulent conscience is not healed by managing itself; it is healed by a change of audience. As M'Cheyne put it: "For every look at self, take ten looks at Christ."

Caution: Peter must not be flattened into a single diagnosis — the man who drew the sword and stepped out of the boat is no melancholic, and not every failure is scruple. That is exactly his usefulness: he is the Bible's fullest biography of a conscience learning to take God's verdict over every other audience — and the demonstration that grace does not first change your temperament; it teaches your temperament to listen to the right voice.

Section Six

The Sufferers (Case Studies)

  • Ignatius of Loyola — nearly destroyed by scruples at Manresa (repeated general confessions, suicidal thoughts); later wrote rules on scruples into the Spiritual Exercises.
  • Martin Luther — see headwater 2. The case where scruple and works-righteousness are shown to be one thing.
  • John Bunyan — Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners: the classic first-person account (obsessive fear of having committed the unpardonable sin; intrusive blasphemous thoughts). Note the arc: the same book opens with his performative-scruple phase (headwater 3, where he serves as patron) — one life, two headwaters, in sequence.
  • Alphonsus Liguori — the great moral theologian was himself lifelong scrupulous; hence his gentleness with the scrupulous.
  • William Cowper — see headwater 4. The case where the bodily/mental stream runs strongest, in a soul of genuine grace.

Pattern worth naming: several of the church's most fruitful teachers on grace learned it inside this affliction.

Section Seven

The Classic Remedies — Where the Whole Tradition Converges

A caveat before using Catholic sources here. In an important sense, the entire Catholic program — confession, penance, direction, the sacramental apparatus — is itself a system of activities offered for assurance. That is not the game Protestants are playing: our assurance rests on a finished work outside us, not on rightly performed activities. So we quote these directors as diagnosticians, not as architects.

And yet — this should be emphasized, not hidden: scrupulosity is such an old problem that it long predates the Reformation's dividing lines, and on the practical handling of the scrupulous conscience, old Catholicism, Anglican casuistry, and Puritan pastoral theology are strikingly aligned. When traditions that disagree about the ground of assurance converge on the same remedies, that convergence is itself evidence about the nature of the disease:

  1. Do not argue with the scruple. Reasoning with it feeds it; the conscience is already sufficiently answered. Taylor: "rudely throw it away," act "according to the sense of his reason, not to the murmurs of his scruple."
  2. Act against the fear. The scrupulous person should proceed in defiance of the misgiving — go to the Table, pray, decide, move.
  3. Submit to one trusted counselor and obey — rather than circulating among many for fresh reassurance.
  4. Refuse the reassurance loop. The counselor answers once, clearly, "unaccompanied by reasons," and does not permit endless re-presentation of doubts.
  5. Tend the body. Melancholy, sleeplessness, solitariness, "a troubled head" — not everything spiritual-feeling has a spiritual cause (headwater 4).
  6. Keep the sufferer near Christ's gentleness, not near rigorist books and morbid self-examination.
  7. Do not ruminate on layers God forbids. Some inquiries are not humble — they are trespass dressed as diligence. Psalm 131: "I do not occupy myself with things too great and too marvelous for me… I have calmed and quieted my soul" — I will not lift my head higher than my pay grade. And Paul makes it concrete at the meat market (1 Cor. 10:25–27): "Eat whatever is set before you, raising no question on the ground of conscience." Note what Paul forbids: not the meat — the question. The scrupulous move is to open a layer of investigation God has expressly told us to leave shut. Refusing to ask is not laxity; it is obedience. (Cf. Deut. 29:29 — "the secret things belong to the LORD.")

Worth noticing: these remedies (don't engage the obsession, act against the fear, block reassurance-seeking, one authoritative counselor) anticipate modern exposure-and-response-prevention with startling precision. The tradition got the phenomenology right centuries before the DSM.

Section Eight

Distinctions Worth Making Crisp

  • Conviction of the Spirit vs. scrupulous accusation. Conviction is specific, scriptural, points to Christ, and finds relief upon repentance. Scruples are vague, unmoored from any actual text, circle endlessly, and are never satisfied by the gospel's answer.
Pastor Chris's Teaching: The King's Cubit

How do you teach the "scriptural" element of that distinction? The cubit. In the ancient world a cubit was the length of a forearm — which meant every man had his own. Left to private forearms, no two houses in the kingdom would measure alike. So the king established a master standard — the royal cubit, cut in stone — and every working rod in the kingdom was carried back to the standard and calibrated against it. Egypt's overseers checked their rods against the granite master regularly; a rod that drifted was corrected, not consulted.

The conscience is a measuring rod, not the standard. It is authoritative in your life the way a foreman's rod is authoritative on the job site — derivatively, only insofar as it has been laid against the King's cubit, the written Word. This cuts in both directions of the taxonomy: a rod worn short excuses real sin (the lax conscience); a rod stretched long condemns what the King never condemned (the scrupulous conscience). Both are calibration failures. "There's a lot of joy being stolen because your conscience is going beyond what is written — it's not calibrated against the king's cubit."

So the scriptural test for any accusation of conscience: carry the rod back to the standard. Can you lay this charge against an actual text? Chapter and verse — not a vibe, not an inference three steps removed, not "a Christian probably shouldn't." Would a well-taught believer read that verse and see your case in it? If yes — that is conviction; repent and be free. If the charge cannot be laid against the standard, the rod has drifted: the measurement is void, and the obedient act is to recalibrate the rod, not to obey it. (1 Cor. 4:6 — "do not go beyond what is written"; Deut. 4:2; Mark 7:7–8 — "teaching as doctrines the commandments of men.")

Note the pastoral gentleness built into the image: a drifted rod isn't a wicked rod. You don't smash it — you teach it. "We don't ravage our consciences. We teach our conscience. We instruct our conscience." Calibration is patient, repeated, and done against something objective that stays still. And this names the goal of caring for the scrupulous: to help them learn how their conscience is reliably off, and in what ways it reliably deviates from the King's cubit — which subjects, which direction, how far. We want them to come to recognize their own tendencies, the way a marksman knows his rifle pulls left.

  • True guilt vs. false guilt.
  • Tender conscience vs. scrupulous conscience. (Tenderness rests when forgiven; scrupulosity cannot.)
  • Binding the conscience where God has left it free — scrupulosity often lives in adiaphora: food, dress, entertainment, degrees of devotion, "did I pray enough?" (Col. 2; Rom. 14.)
  • Severity to the body vs. actual mortification. Col. 2:23 vs. Rom. 8:13 / Col. 3:5 — ascetic rule-keeping has "no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh"; Spirit-wrought mortification is a different thing entirely. The scrupulous person is often working very hard at the one that doesn't work.
  • Humility vs. the appearance of humility. (Headwater 3; Col. 2:18, 23.)
  • Assurance: scrupulosity as a chief enemy of assurance — and why Sibbes aims Christ's gentleness precisely there.

Section Nine

A Field Guide of Forms — Common, Uncommon, and Distinctly Modern

The principle beneath the list: scrupulosity always attaches to a code, and the conscience is perfectly happy to work with codes God never wrote. "Touch not, taste not, handle not" was itself a handling-and-diet code (Col. 2:21); the law-writing faculty never idles (Rom. 2:15). When the culture's code changes, the scruple survives the change — which is why several of the modern forms below don't look "religious" at all.

The classic forms (named briefly):

  • Communion worthiness — perpetual fear of eating and drinking judgment (1 Cor. 11:27–29 misread as a bar to the weak rather than a warning to the flippant)
  • Salvation re-checking — "did I really repent? really believe?" — assurance examined like a stove burner that never stays checked
  • Prayer performance — length, wording, wandering thoughts, missed devotions as disqualifiers
  • Confession loops — re-confessing sins already confessed; the apology that never lands
  • Blasphemous intrusive thoughts / unpardonable-sin fear — Bunyan's territory
  • Will-of-God paralysis — treating choices Scripture leaves free as tests with one hidden right answer
  • Media auditing — endless worldliness litigation over shows, music, books
  • Evangelism guilt — every unshared gospel opportunity as blood on my hands (Ezek. 3 misapplied)

A word of charity before going further. Every one of these classic forms is the distortion of something real. Communion should be approached with self-examination; prayer is worth doing earnestly; God's will is worth seeking; what we watch and hear is worth weighing. These expectations are correct to a certain degree — and incorrect beyond it. And a particular believer may have particular weaknesses that make a stricter personal practice genuinely wise for him. So we read this whole list charitably: the scrupulous person is almost always over-tending something that deserves tending. Of course, the conscience would dearly love to know the secret percentage at which each of these concerns is exactly correct — the precise line where diligence ends and scruple begins. But that is simply not how these things work. There is no published percentage, and demanding one is itself the scruple talking; this is calibration territory, walked by the Spirit, not a formula. Keep all of that in mind as you read the modern and surprising forms below — each of them, too, is guarding something good.

The modern and surprising forms:

  • The Luddite reflex — technology as potential defilement: smartphones, screens, social media, AI. The modern "touch not."
  • Medical purity — homeopathy vs. modern medicine as a moral binary: the pharmaceutical as contamination, the tincture as faith; taking the prescription experienced as unbelief. (Overlaps stream 4 ironically: the scruple can forbid the very treatment the mixture needs.)
  • Food purity — organic, non-GMO, seed oils, raw milk, "clean eating" as moral categories; the crunchy scruple. Its clinical cousin has a name: orthorexia. Indeed, it ought to be noticed that a significant number of the women in our churches today struggled with eating disorders in their late teens and early twenties — we need to be aware that that sort of scrupulosity can shift into something more akin to "crunchy."
  • Parenting scrupulosity — feeding, schooling, screen-time, discipline method as tests of faithfulness; chronic mom-guilt as a false conscience; the conviction that every child outcome traces back to a rule the parent broke.
  • Environmental scruple — carbon guilt, waste auditing, flight shame; ordinary consumption experienced as perpetual trespass.
  • Ethical-consumption scruple — supply-chain auditing before any purchase; the despair loop of "no ethical consumption"; the boycott list as a purity code.
  • Productivity and stewardship scruple — "redeeming the time" turned tyrannical: rest experienced as theft, leisure as guilt, the quantified self (steps, screens, sleep scores) as a daily examen of conscience.
  • Affluence guilt — inability to enjoy any comfort while anyone anywhere suffers; giving that can never discharge the debt because the debt is felt as infinite.
  • Singleness scruples — pressing courtship codes beyond the Scripture.
  • Sexual scruples — difficulty enjoying God's good gift of sexual pleasure within marriage.
  • Speech scruples — pathological fear of having lied; compulsive caveating and correcting; fear that a prayer was worded wrongly and therefore didn't count.
  • Secondary separation — the old independent-fundamentalist doctrine of degrees of separation: not only separating from error (primary), but separating from believers who fail to separate from it (secondary), and on outward from there. Contamination treated as transitive — my thing is connected to someone who is connected to something bad — so the circle of the clean contracts forever. A whole ecclesiology built out of the chain-of-touch scruple.
  • Source purity — the same contagion logic applied to knowledge: the demand that every author, thinker, or tool you learn from be free of moral contamination before its content may be weighed — nearly a bulverism, judging ideas by the moral standing of their origins rather than by their truth. Scripture itself disproves the rule: Paul approvingly cites the Stoic poets to the Areopagus ("in him we live and move and have our being… we are indeed his offspring," Acts 17:28), quotes Epimenides as authority (Titus 1:12), and drops a line of the pagan playwright Menander into the resurrection chapter (1 Cor. 15:33). The apostle's test for a source was whether it said something true, not whether it was clean.

Note the pattern: every entry pairs a genuine good (health, stewardship, justice, discernment, purity) with rule-multiplication — and usually an audience. All five streams run through every form; the form is just the code the scruple happened to grab. Use the list as a recognition checklist: which of these have a grip on you — or on those in your care?

The same mechanics

That poem, written for my wife, is operating on the same basic mechanics as the Song of Solomon. Not the same craft — nobody is confusing it with inspired Scripture — but the same machinery underneath. The Song is not just the canon's warrant for married love; it is the canon's pattern for how married love talks. Put the two side by side and the shared mechanics come out.

Mechanic one: creation is demoted to supporting cast. The Song's most persistent move is to drag the entire created order into the room and rank it below the beloved. She is "fair as the moon, bright as the sun, awesome as an army with banners" (Song 6:10). She is compared to Tirzah and Jerusalem — cities — and wins (6:4). Doves, gazelles, vineyards, towers, Mount Lebanon: the whole landscape is conscripted as her understudy. Fireflies runs the identical move with the polarity flipped — instead of nature furnishing the compliments, nature concedes: fireflies cover their eyes, stars curtsy, the wind is a sigh of defeat, and mother nature herself is runner-up tonight. Either way the mechanic is the same: in the presence of the beloved, everything else in creation is reduced to second place. The refrain says it flat out — you quiet all the pretty things around you, 'cause they aren't you — which is simply Song 6:10 in a Kansas summer backyard.

Mechanic two: hyperbole is the honest register. By any empirical standard, no woman outshines the sun and no smile makes it rain. But love poetry is not reporting measurements; it is reporting rank — the actual order of the lover's world. The Song's extravagance ("your eyes are doves," "your neck is like the tower of David hung with a thousand shields") is not exaggeration lapsing from truth but precision about a different truth: what she is to him. The poem's overreach — trees swaying because she exists — is the genre working as designed. Understatement, in this genre, would be the lie.

Mechanic three: praise is an argument, aimed at her resistance. The bride of the Song deflects — "Do not gaze at me because I am dark, because the sun has looked upon me" (Song 1:6) — and the groom's response is to keep telling her what she is until she believes it: "Behold, you are beautiful, my love, behold, you are beautiful" (1:15; 4:1, 7 — "there is no flaw in you"). The center of Fireflies is the same contest: one thing I'm insisting — oh, stop resisting — the truth that your existing... Love poetry in the Song's lineage is not decoration; it is persuasion, a sustained campaign against the beloved's refusal of her own glory.

Mechanic four: it is relentlessly particular. The Song is not about womankind; it is about one bride, this vineyard, this garden — "my sister, my bride" — with her own name-shaped place in the poem. Fireflies is not "a love poem"; it is for Angela, about one specific summer night, one specific head of hair in the moonlight. The genre only works aimed at one person. Generalized, it dies.

Why husbands should write verse

This page sits in the wiki for a practical reason. The Song is Eden's song sung in Jerusalem — the garden's music carried out through the gate — and every husband is invited to sing it again in his own yard.

How do you tend a garden-wife?

With words, of course. If the wife is the garden within the garden, the obvious question is what the watering can holds — and Ephesians 5 tells us the secret. The Bridegroom sanctifies his bride "by the washing of water with the word" (Eph 5:26). Christ's tending instrument is verbal. He does not renovate his bride with programs or pressure; he washes her with what he says to her, and the washing ends in splendor.

And once you've seen it in Ephesians, you see it everywhere — above all in the Song itself. Notice what the Song of Solomon actually is: almost nothing happens in it. There is no plot to speak of, no wedding logistics, no domestic scenes. It is eight chapters of nearly unbroken address — two lovers describing, praising, inviting, answering. The Song of Solomon is a wife-tending book, and the tending is the talking. The groom walks his garden the way a gardener walks his rows, and what he pours out on every bed he passes is speech: behold, you are beautiful; there is no flaw in you; let me hear your voice.

What kind of words?

Mostly, overwhelmingly, words of affirming love. But say the whole truth: does Christ tend his garden-wife with some hard words — words of warning, of admonishment, of rebuke? Of course he does. See the seven churches of Revelation 2–3: "I have this against you"; "repent, or else." See Christ speaking through Paul to the Galatians — "O foolish Galatians, who has bewitched you?" — and to the Corinthians, letter after corrective letter. A gardener who never prunes is not tending; he is neglecting with a smile. The hard words are real tending too.

But watch the proportions. Even the seven letters of Revelation open with "I know your works" and commendation before any charge is filed, and every one of them ends with a promise to the conqueror. Even the Corinthians — the messiest church in the New Testament — get a letter that begins "I give thanks to my God always for you" (1 Cor 1:4). Galatians stands nearly alone in skipping the opening thanksgiving, and it is famous precisely for the omission — the exception loud enough to prove the rule. And when the prophets pull back the curtain on how the Lord speaks over his bride, this is what they report: "He will rejoice over you with gladness; he will quiet you by his love; he will exult over you with loud singing" (Zephaniah 3:17). "As the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so shall your God rejoice over you" — and he renames her Hephzibah, "My Delight Is in Her" (Isaiah 62:4–5). The Lord of the church writes verse over his wife. He sings, audibly, at volume.

So the ratio is the lesson. Rebuke has its place in the watering can, but the overwhelming use of words by our Lord for his wife is words of joy and satisfaction and celebration — and husbands imitating the Bridegroom should hit roughly his mix. If the hard words are the only verse your wife ever hears from you, you are not pruning a garden; you are running a courtroom in a greenhouse.

C. S. Lewis's line, already load-bearing elsewhere in this wiki, applies here with full force: praise is inner health made audible, the completion of enjoyment. A husband who never puts his delight into words has not finished delighting. The Song of Solomon is eight chapters of the delight finished. Fireflies is one summer night's attempt at the same.

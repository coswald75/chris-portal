Ordinary Transmutation

Part of the Crown Each Day wiki.

Definition: Ordinary transmutation is the tending of basic things which, through a series of unglamorous processes, creates something golden. God does not hand over the golden things of life directly — wine, oil, bread, gladness, a shining face. He gives grass, plants, and the capacity to work, and the gold comes into being through cultivation, patience, and process. The alchemists were looking for the wrong miracle in the wrong place. The real transmutation is ordinary, and it happens at home.

Alchemy has been around forever

Isaac Newton — the man who gave us calculus, the laws of motion, and the clockwork of the heavens — spent more of his life hunched over crucibles than most people suspect. Behind his rooms at Trinity College he kept furnaces that burned for weeks at a stretch, and he left behind something like a million words of alchemical notes: recipes for the philosopher's stone, meditations on "philosophick mercury," diagrams of the star regulus of antimony. He worked bare-handed with quicksilver and recorded in his notebooks how his preparations tasted — centuries later, analysis of his surviving hair found it loaded with mercury, which may explain the year he spent writing paranoid letters to his friends. When John Maynard Keynes bought a trunk of Newton's private papers in 1936 and actually read them, he came away shaken: Newton, he announced, was not the first of the age of reason but "the last of the magicians."

And Newton was a latecomer to the guild. A generation before him, a Hamburg alchemist named Hennig Brand, convinced that gold's essence must reside in the body's golden fluid, collected urine by the barrel — some fifteen hundred gallons of it, stockpiled, putrefying, boiled down to a black paste in a process whose smell can be left to the imagination — and got, instead of gold, a waxy substance that glowed in the dark: phosphorus, dug out of the least dignified matter on earth. Long before Brand, Chinese emperors were drinking elixirs of immortality compounded from cinnabar and mercury, and dying of them, one dynasty after another failing to notice the pattern. The word itself is old past tracing: alchemy, from the Arabic al-kīmiyā, reaching back — some say — to Khem, the black land, Egypt, where the whole enterprise may have begun among embalmers and metalworkers before anyone thought to write it down.

Alchemy has been around forever, in other words, and it has always been the same wager: that base matter hides something golden, and that the right process, faithfully executed, will bring it out. Centuries of burned fingers, poisoned bodies, and boiled urine testify that the instinct runs deeper than the failures. The alchemists were not wrong about the wager. They were wrong about the laboratory. There is a process that turns base things golden — the Bible describes it in detail — and it was running on every farm and in every kitchen within walking distance of Newton's furnace, the whole time.

The psalm's grammar

Psalm 104 praises God for the golden trio:

You cause the grass to grow for the livestock and plants for man to cultivate, that he may bring forth food from the earth and wine to gladden the heart of man, oil to make his face shine and bread to strengthen man's heart. (Psalm 104:14–15)

Read it slowly and notice what God is actually said to give. He causes grass to grow. He causes plants to grow — "for man to cultivate, that he may bring forth food from the earth and wine... oil... bread." The he who brings forth the wine, the oil, and the bread is the man. God's direct gifts in this verse are raw: green things growing out of dirt. The finished goods — the ones that gladden hearts, brighten faces, and strengthen bodies — arrive at the end of a chain the psalm deliberately routes through human tending.

So in one sense, God doesn't give those things. Grapes grow; wine doesn't. Olives grow; oil doesn't. Wheat grows; bread doesn't. Nobody has ever picked a loaf.

Grapes grow; wine doesn't

Consider what stands between each raw gift and its golden form. Between the grape and the wine: a kept vineyard, a harvest, a crushing, a long fermentation in the dark, and time you cannot rush. Between the olive and the oil: years of tending before a tree bears at all, then beating, then pressing — the oil only comes out under weight. Between the wheat and the bread: plowing, sowing, waiting, reaping, threshing, grinding, kneading, fire. Every step is ordinary. No single step is impressive. And the sum of the steps is transmutation: dirt-colored basics turned golden — amber in the cup, gold in the cruse, brown-gold at the crust.

This is where the alchemists went wrong. Newton and Brand wanted the transmutation in one arcane stroke — a stone, a secret, an essence. The Bible's economy is the opposite: no stroke, no arcana — just series. Tend the basic thing, run the process faithfully, and the base becomes golden. The miracle is real; it is simply distributed across a hundred boring faithful acts. That is why it gets missed.

The gift is still God's

None of this removes God from the giving — it relocates the giving. The old Jewish table blessing is careful and correct: "Blessed are you, LORD our God, King of the universe, who brings forth bread from the earth." The earth brings forth wheat; the blessing credits God with the bread — because the whole chain, the grain and the hands and the fire and the skill, is his provision. The one time God did skip the chain and drop bread straight from heaven, it was an emergency ration, and it stopped the day Israel ate the produce of the land (Joshua 5:12). Manna is the exception that proves the economy: the normal way God gives bread is through people making it.

From the Hall — Do Christianity (2023-12-03, quoting Joe Rigney): "Things we taste — the warm sweetness of chocolate chip cookies... the piercing saltiness of well-seasoned meat... You need to understand that there's a person who thought all that up, and that's your God. He invented that stuff. It didn't exist and he made it."

God invented the pleasures and the processes. Ordinary transmutation is not man supplementing a stingy God; it is the designed delivery mechanism of a generous one — the same design as Eden itself, where the garden was given and the man was put in it to work it.

The transmutation ends in persons

Look once more at where Psalm 104 actually terminates. Not at the wine — at the gladdened heart. Not at the oil — at the shining face. Not at the bread — at the strengthened heart. The golden products of the process are not finally on the table; they are in the people around it. Wine, oil, and bread are themselves intermediate steps in a longer transmutation whose end products are gladness, brightness, and strength.

And these are exactly the three items of Ecclesiastes 9:7–8 — "eat your bread with joy, drink your wine with a merry heart... let not oil be lacking on your head." The things you crown the day with are transmuted things: the fruit of your labor, literally fermented, pressed, and baked. This is also the deep logic of the walled garden: the home is the workshop where ordinary transmutation runs — where tended basics (meals, order, sleep, prayers, the same table every evening) compound, through a series of processes, into the golden things no one can buy: gladness, safety, shining faces. Nothing about the inputs is impressive. That is the point. Gold, here, is not found. It is made — slowly, ordinarily, at home.

The page in one line

God gives grass, grapes, olives, and wheat — the golden things (wine, oil, bread, and finally gladness, shining faces, and strength) come into being through ordinary transmutation: basic things tended through a series of faithful processes, mostly at home.

Home as Walled Garden

Part of the Crown Each Day wiki.

Definition: The home is meant to be a walled garden — a safe, ordered place for the cultivation of your most essential work. The wall runs both directions: it guards what is inside from the chaos outside, and it marks the ground where your care is actually supposed to be spent. A walled garden does not need to be grand. It needs to be kept.

The garden had walls

Eden was not wilderness; it was a garden — which is to say, bounded ground under care. Adam's commission was two verbs: "The LORD God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to work it and keep it" (Genesis 2:15) — avad, cultivate, and shamar, guard. Cultivation and protection are one job. Ground that is worked but not guarded gets overrun; ground that is guarded but not worked is just a fence around weeds. The Song of Songs later reaches for the same image as the picture of covenanted intimacy: "a garden locked is my sister, my bride" (Song 4:12) — the hortus conclusus, the enclosed garden, beautiful precisely because it is set apart.

A home that does Eden anyway takes both verbs seriously. It is the plot of ground this family has been given to work and to keep.

Incidentally: a walled garden has walls — and a gate. Which means not everyone is allowed in, and no one is allowed in all the time. The wall is not a contradiction of welcome; it is what makes welcome possible, because only a kept garden has something to open. This will come back into play when we discuss Hospitality.

The Augustinian heat map

Why does the home deserve this level of care, when the world outside is so loudly falling apart? Because you are a creature with a limited budget of love, and the Bible tells you where to spend it.

From the Hall — The Earthy Piety of Providence (2026-08-09, Ecclesiastes 9:7–10): "Because God is actually in control, we can start thinking differently about how to process the uncertainty and the randomness — which leads me to the next section, which is what I refer to as the Augustinian heat map."

The sermon builds the image from a 2019 study (Adam Waytz's team, with Jonathan Haidt) in which people were given a hundred "credits of care" to spend across concentric circles running from family and city out to universal, planetary concerns. Some reliably piled their credits at the center — their actual family, their actual community, their actual city. Others reliably spent them on the outermost rings.

From the Hall — The Earthy Piety of Providence (2026-08-09): "What Ecclesiastes is doing is he's saying, no — the one on the left is right. That's the correct version. And that's because you don't have the ability to take care of everything. You're a creature. You have a limited ability to love. And therefore you must focus on those things which are in proximity to you. And the reason I call it Augustinian is because long before all of this ever happened, when Augustine was writing On Christian Doctrine, he says this: 'All men are to be loved equally; but since you cannot do good to all, you are to pay special regard to those who, by the accidents of time or place or circumstance, are brought into closer connection with you.' He doesn't mean accidents in the real fundamental way. He means it in the providential way."

From the Hall — The Earthy Piety of Providence (2026-08-09): "And that's the preacher's answer to the perceived uncertainty. He keeps bringing us back to our house. He keeps bringing us back to our meals, to our worship, to our walk with God, to our spouse, to the people we've committed to, to our church. He keeps bringing us back there."

On the heat map, the home is the hottest square — the ground brought closest to you by providence. That is what makes it the site of your most essential work: the walk with God, the marriage, the children, the table where the day is crowned. The walled garden is not a retreat from your calling. It is the center of it.

Not a trophy case of delayed decisions

If the home is where the essential work happens, the environment either serves that work or taxes it. Which is where clutter comes in.

Barbara Hemphill's definition has become the standard one: "Clutter is postponed decisions." Every pile is a stack of choices you declined to make — keep or toss, file or act, repair or release. A cluttered home is therefore a trophy case of delayed decisions: the backlog put on display, shelf after shelf of deferred verdicts, each one quietly announcing that things here are not dealt with. Nobody rests in a room that is still arguing with them.

Ordering the home is simply making the decisions. That is why it belongs in this wiki rather than in a housekeeping manual: the discipline of the walled garden is decisiveness exercised on your own square footage — shamar, the keeping half of Adam's job.

Used furniture, clean floors

None of this requires money. The walled garden does not have to be fancy; every piece of furniture can be used. The standard is not impressiveness but order: clean, organized, kept. The test of the room is what it says when you walk into it. A trophy case of delayed decisions says you are behind. A walled garden — however plain — says you are safe here, and you can rest.

That message is the precondition for everything else in this wiki. The Evening Routine happens somewhere, and the somewhere preaches. The crown of the day is easiest to set in a room that is already at peace.

The Irish have a farewell that names the whole aspiration: Slán abhaile — "safe home." It is what you say to someone leaving, and notice what it assumes: that the journey's proper end is not merely arrival but safety, and that the place waiting at the end of the road is the kind of place that supplies it. Every member of a household walks out the gate each morning into the thorns and the dust. The walled garden is the answer to the blessing — the home that makes slán abhaile come true every evening it is spoken. (Whether yours actually does is a question worth auditing: see Slán Abhaile.)

The page in one line

The home is a walled garden — bounded, guarded, cultivated ground at the center of the Augustinian heat map — and its order need not be fancy, only clean and decided, so the room itself says: you are safe, and you can rest.
